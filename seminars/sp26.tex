\section{January 30: Multiplicity Formulae for Spherical Varieties}
This talk was given by Toan Pham at Johns Hopkins University for the student automorphic representations seminar.

\subsection{The Theorems}
Fix a homogeneous spherical variety $X=G/H$ over a local field $F$. Also, choose a character $\chi\colon H(F)\to\CC^\times$ and an irreducible representation $\pi$ of $G(F)$. Then the local relative Langlands program is interested in the multiplicity
\[m(\pi,\chi)\coloneqq\dim\op{Hom}_{H(F)}(\pi,\chi).\]
Note that
\[m(\pi,\chi)=\dim\op{Hom}_{G(F)}(\pi,C^\infty(X(F),\chi)).\]
Here are some starting examples.
\begin{example}[Whittaker] \label{ex:whittaker}
	An interesting case is when $H\subseteq G$ is a unipotent subgroup. For example, one can take the unipotent radical $U$ of the Borel subgroup of $\op{GL}_2$. Then we can take $\chi$ to be lifted from any additive character $F\to\CC^\times$. One can generalize this example to work from any $\mf{sl}_2$-triple, and it produces Whittaker models of $\pi$ in $C^\infty(X(F),\chi)$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}[Gan--Gross--Prasad] \label{ex:ggp}
	Fix a quadratic extension $E/F$. Then one can take $X={\op{SO}_n}\backslash(\mathrm{SO}_n\times\mathrm{SO}_{n+1})$ or $X=\mathrm U_n\backslash(\mathrm U_n\times\mathrm U_{n+1})$. In general, there are more examples arising from so-called ``GGP triples'' $(G,H,\chi)$.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that $m(\pi,\chi)\le1$ in \Cref{ex:whittaker,ex:ggp}, but this is not always true.
\end{remark}
Nonetheless, it becomes interesting to discover when $m(\pi,\chi)\ge1$. Here are some answers.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix everything as in \Cref{ex:whittaker,ex:ggp} and choose a Langlands parameter $\varphi\colon W_F\to {^LG}$. Then the Langlands packet $\Pi_\varphi$ contains exactly one $\pi$ for which $m(\pi,\chi)\ne0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix everything as in \Cref{ex:ggp}, and choose a Langlands parameter $\varphi\colon W_F\to {^LG}$. Then $\pi\in\Pi_\varphi$ has $m(\pi,\chi)\ne0$ if and only if it maps to a speified unitary character.
\end{theorem}
Our method will be the so-called ``local trace formulae,'' developed originally by Waldspurger.

\subsection{Local Trace Formulae}
For the rest of the talk, we work in the context of \Cref{ex:ggp}. The type of result we are trying to prove rewrites $m(\pi,\chi)$ in terms of a sum of orbital integrals.
\begin{example}
	For finite groups $G$, we can use the character $\Theta_\pi$ of $\pi$ to see that
	\[m(\pi,\chi)=\sum_{\text{conj. }[x]\subseteq H}\frac1{\#C_H(x)}\cdot\Theta_\pi(x)\ov\chi(h).\]
	This right-hand side can be viewed as some twisted sum of sizes of conjugacy classes.
\end{example}
Our method to prove such formulae will rest on local trace formulae, which basically amount to finding two ways of expressing the trace of some $f\in C_c^\infty(G)$ acting on $L^p(H(F)\backslash G(F),\chi)$. On one hand, we may write
\begin{align*}
	(Rf\cdot\varphi)(x) &= \int_{G(F)}f(g)\varphi(xg)\,dg \\
	&= \int_{H(F)\backslash G(F)}K_f(x,y)\varphi(y)\,dy,
\end{align*}
where $K_f(x,y)=\int_{H(F)}f\left(x^{-1}hy\right)\chi(h)\,dh$. We morally then expect the trace of $Rf$ to be the sum of $K_f$ along the diagonal, as one finds with finite groups.
\begin{proposition}
	Fix everything as above. Say that $f\in C_c^\infty(G(F))$ is strongly cuspidal if and only if
	\[\int_{U(F)}f(um)\,du=0\]
	for any parabolic $P$ with Levi decomposition $P=MU$. If $f$ is strongly cuspidal, then the trace of $Rf$ converges absolutely.
\end{proposition}
The local trace formula now amounts to expressing $J(f)$ either via a spectral or a geometric expansion.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The spectral expansion is
	\[J_{\mathrm{spec}}(f)\coloneqq\int_{\mf X(G)}D(\pi)\widehat\Theta_f(\pi)m(\pi)\,d\pi.\]
	Here, $\mf X(G)$ consists of the space of tempered representations (i.e., found in $L^2(G)$) arising from para\-bolic induction of elliptic representations. Then $\Theta_f$ is a weighted orbital ingeral of $f$, and $\widehat\Theta_f$ is its Fourier transform. The $D(\pi)$ is unknown, but we have been reassured that it is not important.

	The idea to show that $J(f)=J_{\mathrm{spec}}(f)$ is to use the Plancheral formula
	\[\langle f_1,f_2\rangle=\int_{\widehat G}J_\pi(f_1\otimes f_2)\,d_X\pi,\]
	where $J_\pi$ is the natural composite
	\[C_c^\infty(X\times X)\to\pi\otimes\widetilde\pi\to\CC.\]
	It turns out that $J_\pi$ is non-vanishing if and only if $m(\pi,\chi)\ne0$. Yiannis claims that the forward direction is easy.

	\item The geometric expansion associates a quasicharacter $\theta_f\colon G_{\mathrm{reg}}(F)\to\CC$ defined on the regular semi\-simple locus, and then we have
	\[m_{\mathrm{geom}}(\theta)\coloneqq\lim_{s\to0}\int_{\Gamma(G,H)}D^G(x)^{1/2}c_\theta(x)\Delta^{s-1/2}\,dx.\]
	Here, $\Gamma(G,H)$ is the space of semisimple conjugacy classes of $G(F)$ which are represented by an element in $H(F)$, and $c_\theta$ is some extension of $\theta$ to the semisimple locus $G_{\mathrm{ss}}(F)$. Our geometric expansion is then $J_{\mathrm{geom}}(f)\coloneqq m_{\mathrm{geom}}(\Theta_f)$.
\end{itemize}
It now turns out that
\[J_{\mathrm{spec}}(f)=J(f)=J_{\mathrm{geom}}(f),\]
though both of these equalities are theorems. Though it requires an argument, this turns out to be equivalent to a multiplicity formula $m_{\mathrm{geom}}(\pi)=m(\pi)$.
\begin{conj}
	One has $m_{\mathrm{geom}}(\pi)=m(\pi)$ for any spherical $X$.
\end{conj}

\section{January 31: Finiteness Theorems in Arithmetic Statistics}
This talk was given by Fatemehzahra Janbazi for the JHUnior number theory days.

\subsection{The Birch--Merriman Theorems}
For today, $K$ will be a number field, and $S$ is a finite set of places, including the infinite ones.
\begin{notation}
	For a ring $R$, we define $U_n(R)$ to be the set of binary forms $f$ which are homogeneous of degree $n$.
\end{notation}
Note that $\op{GL}_2$ naturally acts on $\op U_n$.
\begin{definition}
	There is also a discriminant $\Delta$ on $U_n$. We say that $f\in\op U_n(\QQ_p)$ has \textit{good reduction} if and only if $\Delta(f)\in\OO_p^\times$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	The discriminant satisfies
	\[\Delta(\gamma\cdot f)=\det\gamma^{n(n-1)}\Delta(f).\]
\end{remark}
We then have the following theorems.
\begin{theorem}[Birch--Merriman]
	The set $U_n(\OO_{K,S})$ with good reduction outside $S$ breaks into infinitely many $\op{GL}_2(\OO_{K,S})$-orbits.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	For any nonzero $D\in\OO_K$, the set of forms in $U_n(\OO_{K})$ with disciminant $D$ has finitely many equivalence classes for the action of $\op{GL}_2(\OO_K)$.
\end{theorem}
We are interested in analogous results for ternary forms.
\begin{definition}
	For a ring $R$, we define $V_n(R)$ to be the set of homogeneous ternary forms of degree $n$. There is also a discriminant $\Delta$ of ternary forms, and we say that $f$ has \textit{good reduction} at $p$ if and only if $\Delta(f)\in\OO_p^\times$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	As usual, there is a natural $\op{GL}_3$-action on $V_n$, and it satisfies
	\[\Delta(\gamma\cdot f)=\det\gamma^{n(n-1)}\Delta(f).\]
\end{remark}
Here are our main theorems.
\begin{theorem} \label{thm:vn-finiteness}
	The set of elements in $V_n(\OO_{K,S})$ with good reduction outside $S$ breaks up into finitely many $\op{GL}_3(\OO_{K,S})$-orbits.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	For any nonzero $D\in\OO_K$, the set of forms in $V_n(\OO_K)$ with discrimimant $D$ has finitely many equivalence classes for the action of $\op{GL}_3(\OO_K)$.
\end{theorem}
Here is another analogue.
\begin{definition}
	For a ring $R$, we define
	\[V_{2,2}(R)\coloneqq\frac{R[x_1,x_2,x_3]_2\otimes_RR[z_1,z_2,z_3]_2}{\left(\sum_ix_iz_i\right)\cap R[x_1,x_2,x_3]_2\otimes_RR[z_1,z_2,z_3]_2}.\]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	There is now a natural action of $\op{GL}_3$ on $V_{2,2}$ given by $(\gamma\cdot f)(x,z)\coloneqq f(x\gamma,z\delta\gamma)$.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
	A $K3$ surface $S$ is \textit{generic} if and only if it is smooth and cover $\PP^2$ via a natural projection from a flag variety. Some $f\in V_{2,2}$ has \textit{good reduction} if and only if it products a generic $K3$ surface.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}
	The set of elements in $V_{2,2}(\OO_{K,S})$ with good reduction outside $S$ and Picard rank $2$ breaks up into finitely many orbits for the action of $\op{GL}_2(\OO_{K,S})$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Let's sketch the idea of \Cref{thm:vn-finiteness}. Let $T_n(K,S)$ be the set of $f$ with discriminant in $\OO_{K,S}^\times$. Each such $f$ cuts out a curve $C_f$ which has good reduction outside $S$, but Faltings's theorem implies that there are only finitely many such curves, which can be enumerated by some invariants $I_1(f),\ldots,I_k(f)$. One can now chase around to prove the theorem.
\end{remark}
Here is an application.
\begin{example}
	Let $W_n(R)$ be the set of pairs $(F,G)$ of elements in $V_n$. This has a natural action of ${\op{GL}_2}\times{\op{GL}_3}$ given by
	\[(\gamma_2,\gamma_3)\cdot(F,G)=(\gamma_3F,\gamma_3G)\gamma_2^\intercal.\]
	One can prove a similar result about finiteness of elements in $W_n(\OO_K)$ with fixed discriminant. The proof uses both the finitenesses of $U_n$ and $V_n$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Geometric Reformulation}
We will want more notation.
\begin{notation}
	Let $U_n(K,S)$ be the set of elements in $U_n(\OO_{K,S})$ and $\Delta(f)\in\OO_{K,S}^\times$ and which split over $K$. We also let $\Omega_n(\PP^1;S)$ to be the set of $n$ points with good reduction outside $S$.
\end{notation}
The point of introducing these sets is that there is a natural map $U_n(K,S)\to\Omega_n(\PP^1;S)$, preserving the $\op{GL}_2$-action, and finiteness theorems can be moved from one side to the other.

Thus, we see that we may be interested in analogous questions for curves other than $\PP^1$.
\begin{theorem} \label{thm:finite-good-pts}
	Fix a smooth projective curve $C$ over $K$ with good redution at $S$. One can define $\Omega_n(C;S)$ in the same way. For any $n\ge1$, the set of classes in $\Omega_n(C;S)$ up to the action by $\op{Aut}_{\OO_{K,S}}(\mc C)$ is finite.
\end{theorem}
\begin{example}
	For $C=\PP^1$ and $n=1$, finiteness is equivalent to finiteness of the class group of $\OO_{K,S}$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	If $C=\PP^1$ and $n\ge3$, we recover the Birch--Merriman theorems. Alternatively, one can move some points to $\{0,1,\infty\}$, and then the remaining points are seen to be finite by the unit equation.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	If $C$ has genus $1$, then we have an elliptic curve; moving some point to the origin, we recover Siegel's theorem.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Lastly, if $C$ has genus $2$, then at $n=1$, we recover Faltings's theorem.
\end{example}
The proof of \Cref{thm:finite-good-pts} more or less upgrades the above examples by doing some Galois cohomology.

\section{January 31: Infinitely Many Supersingular Primes}
This talk as given by Fangu Chen at Johns Hopkins University for the JHUnior number theory days.

\subsection{Expectations for Supersingular Primes}
Here are our definitions for elliptic curves.
\begin{definition}[ordinary, supersingular]
	Fix an elliptic curve $E$ over a finite field of characteristic $p$. Then $E$ is \textit{ordinary} if and only if $E[p]\cong\ZZ/p\ZZ$; it is \textit{supersingular} if and only if $E[p]=0$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	There are equivalent formulations based on the Frobenius action or a calculation of the endomorphism ring.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that every elliptic curve is ordinary or supersingular.
\end{remark}
\begin{ques}
	Fix an elliptic curve $E$ over $\QQ$. For how many primes $p$ are there with $E_{\FF_p}$ admit supersingular reduction?
\end{ques}
Here is a heuristic argument: one knows $\left|\tr\mathrm{Frob}_p\right|\le2\sqrt p$, so one can expect that $p$ is supersingular with probability $\sim1/\sqrt p$.
\begin{conj}[Lang--Trotter]
	Fix an elliptic curve $E$ over $\QQ$ without complex multiplication. Then the number of primes $p<X$ for which $E_{\FF_p}$ has supersingular reduction is asymptotic to $c_E\sqrt x/\log x$ for some constant $c_E$.
\end{conj}
For example, Serre has proved that the set of supersingular primes has density $0$; Elkies has proved that there are infinitely many.

We are interested in analogous questions for abelian varieties.
\begin{remark}
	In the case of complex multiplication, it is not so hard to study supersingularity via the Newton polygon and the Shimura--Taniyama formula.
\end{remark}
One expects that the non-ordinary abelian varieties (measured via the Newton polygon) should have density zero. Recent work has shown that the supersingular primes has density zero. We are interested in showing infinitude.

\subsection{The Main Theorem}
For our talk, we fix a totally real number field $F$ as our base and a quaternion algebra $B$ over $F$ which is split at exactly one real palce. Then let $V$ be the subset of $B$ with reduced trace equal to zero, and let $Q_F$ be the reduced norm of $V$.

We will require all these to admit $F$-multiplication. In the case $[F:\QQ]=3$, Mumford has defined some abelian fourfolds with endomorphism algebra $\ZZ$ but admitting some extra Hodge classes in $\mathrm H^4(A;\QQ)$. There is a Kuga--Satake construction which takes $K3$ surfaces to abelian varieties; it turns out that $A^{64}$ is isogenous to an abelian variety coming from a $K3$ surface.
\begin{remark}
	We have recently computed some of these fourfolds as coming from some explicit Jacobians.
\end{remark}
In the case that $F$ has narrow class number $1$, the Shimura curve parameterizing Mumford's abelian fourfolds admits a canonical model $\mathrm{Sh}$.
\begin{theorem}
	Assume that $\mathrm{Sh}$ is isomorphic to $\PP^1_F$ as well as some technical conditions. If $A$ is in $\mathrm{Sh}$ and has field of moduli $F$, then $A$ admits infinitely many primes of supersingular reduction.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	The extra conditions are as follows.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For $F$: we want $2$ to be inert in $F$ and $F(\sqrt{-\varepsilon})$ to have class number $1$ for all $\varepsilon\in\OO_F^\times$ which is negative at exactly one real place $F$. This condition simplifies calculations at the archimedean place; some case-by-case analysis could relax this condition.
		\item For $B$: there are also some ramification conditions.
	\end{itemize}
	There are many examples of such $F$; for example, there are cubic fields.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	Such extensions of Elkies's theorem have a long history. For example, there has been a lot of work on abelian surfaces with some quaternionic multiplication. Notably, all previous work has been done with Shimura curves of PEL type, isomorphic to $\PP^1$. This work is notable because the underlying Shimura curve merely has Hodge type.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Proof Idea}
Let's review the proof idea for elliptic curves, due to Elkies.
\begin{theorem}[Elkies]
	Fix an elliptic curve $E$ over $\QQ$. Then $E$ admits supersingular reduction at infinitely many primes $p$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Suppose $E$ has finitely many supersingular primes in a set $S$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item We start with some CM points. Fix CM elliptic curves $\{E_1,\ldots,E_h\}$ with $\ZZ[\sqrt{-\ell}]\subseteq\op{End}_{\ov\QQ}(E_\bullet)$, where $\ell\equiv-1\pmod4$ is prime. Then we set
		\[P_\ell(x)=\prod_i(x-j(E_i)),\]
		and it turns out to be in $\ZZ[x]$. We would like to consttruct $\ell$ for which $\nu_p(P_\ell(j(E)))>0$ (i.e., $E$ has supersinglar reduction) and $\left(\frac{-\ell}p\right)\ne1$ (i.e., $E_i\pmod p$ has supersingular reduction). Accordingly, let $N$ be the numerator of $P_\ell(j(E))$, and then we have two cases.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item We may want $\ell\mid N$, in which case we choose $p=\ell$.
			\item We may want $\left(\frac{-\ell}N\right)=-1$, in which case we can still find some $p$.
			\item Lastly, we may want $p\notin S$, given from $\left(\frac{-\ell}q\right)=_1$ for all $q\in S$.
		\end{itemize}
		We will achieve these many congruence conditions by some explicit analysis at finite and real places.

		\item Now, at finite places, we may want $\left(\frac N\ell\right)=+1$. The point is that CM liftings are paired, so one finds that $\deg P_\ell(x)$ is even. It follows that $P_\ell(x)$ is a square$\pmod\ell$.

		\item At the real place, one shows that the roots of $P_\ell$ are paired, and then we get a non-square at $N$.
	\end{enumerate}
	The proof is now completed upon some local calculations with quadratic reciprocity.
\end{proof}
Our proof uses the following inputs.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item CM points: these are parameterized by some conjugacy classes of embeddings of CM extensions into $B$. We then construct $P_\lambda(x)$ and do a quadratic reciprocity argument as before.
	\item Finite places: we use something about integral models of Shimura varieties and do pairings as before.
	\item Real places: we use something about Hecke equidistributino.
\end{enumerate}

\section{January 31: \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-adic Higher Green's Functions}
This talk was given by Hazem Hassan for JHUnior number theory days.

\subsection{Higher Green's Functions}
We begin with a story of complex multiplication. Let $\mc H$ be the complex upper-half plane, and let $j\colon\mc H\to\CC$ is the $j$-invariant.
\begin{definition}
	A \textit{CM point} $\tau$ is a point in $\mc H$ such that $\QQ(\tau)$ is an imaginary quadratic field.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a CM point $\tau\in\mc H$. Then $\QQ(\tau,j(\tau))$ is an abelian extension of $\QQ(\tau)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	In fact, the theory of complex multiplication allows us to constuct all abelian extensions of $\QQ(\tau)$ by working only a little harder.
\end{remark}
We now introduce some modular forms.
\begin{example}
	Here are some modular forms of weight $0$.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The function $j-j(\tau)$ is a holomorphic modular form of weight $0$. Here, ``holomorphic'' is on $\mc H$, not $\PP^1_\CC$.
		\item The function $\log(j-j(\tau))$ is a multivalued holomorphic modular form of weight $0$.
		\item The function $\log\left|j-j(\tau)\right|$ is a real analyti cfunction of weight $0$.
		\item One can also take a logarithmic derivative $d\log(j-j(\tau))$, which is still holomorphic but now of weight $2$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}
There are also higher-weight versions of some of these.
\begin{example}
	Fix some even $k\ge4$, and set $n\coloneqq k-2$. Then
	\[f_{k,\tau}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{\gamma\in\op{SL}_2(\ZZ)}\left(\frac{\gamma\tau-\gamma\ov\tau}{(z-\gamma\tau)(z-\gamma\ov\tau)}\right)^{k/2}\]
	is a meromorphic modular form of weight $k$ with (simple) poles on $\op{SL}_2(\ZZ)\cdot\tau$. We cannot evaluate this at our CM points because of the poles, so we would like a primitive.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	We want a notion of differentiation to say something about $d\log$. However, differentiation is not totally $\op{SL}_2$-invariant: if $f$ has weight $-n$, then one finds
	\[d^{n+1}(f|_{-n}\gamma)=\left(d^{n+1}f\right)|_{-k},\]
	where $\cdot|_\bullet$ is the twisted action associated with a modular form.
\end{remark}
There is now a weight $-n$ modular form $\widetilde f_{k,\tau}(z)$ which is a multivalued modular form of weight $-n$; it is constructed via some repeated integration process. We may now define higher Green's functions.
\begin{definition}[higher Green's function]
	We define
	\[G_{k/2}(\tau,\sigma)\coloneqq\Re\delta_{-n}^{n/2}\widetilde f_{k,\tau}(\sigma),\]
	where $\delta_\ell\coloneqq\frac d{dz}+\frac\ell{z-\ov z}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Gross--Zagier conjecture]
	Fix a normalized weakly holomorphic modular form $f$ of weight $-n$ with Laurent expansion $f=\sum_mc(m)q^m$. Then for CM points $\tau$ and $\sigma$,
	\[\sum_{m>0}c(-m)m^{n/2}(T_m\cdot G_{k/2})(\tau,\sigma)\in\ov\QQ.\]
\end{theorem}
These numbers turn out to be intersections of Heegner cycles, which is why we may expect them to be algebraic (due to the standard conjectures). This is now known to Bruinier--Li--Yang, using regularized $\theta$-lifts.

\subsection{Real Multiplication}
We now try to tell a similar story for real multiplication, retelling the story of extensions of real quadratic fields via some geometric picture.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The analogue of $\mc H$ is the $p$-adic upper half-plane $\mc H_p\coloneqq\PP^1(\CC_p)\setminus\PP^1(\QQ_p)$.
	\item The analogue of $\op{SL}_2(\ZZ)$ is $\Gamma\coloneqq\op{SL}_2(\ZZ[1/p])$.
	\item Modular forms come from some sections $\mathrm H^0(\op{SL}_2(\ZZ);\mc M_k)$ of a line bundle. It turns out that the right analogue is $\mathrm H^1(\Gamma;\mc M_k)$, where we go up in a degree to see some modular symbols.
	\item The Heegner points admit some archimedean intersection numbers $\log\left|j(\tau)-j(\sigma)\right|$. In the $p$-adic upper half-plane, one has some Stark--Heegner points, but they only conjecturally lie in the real quadratic field. There is something known about algebraicity.
	\item Heegner cycles have some interesting higher Green's functions. We are interested in putting something on the real multiplication side called ``Stark--Heegner cycles.''
\end{itemize}
We cannot define Stark--Heegner cycles, but we can say something about their conjectural intersections, which should be $p$-adic higher Green's functions.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix an RM point $\tau\in\mc H_p$ and some integer $k\ge2$. Then there is a unique rigid meromorphic cocycle (up to analytic cocycles and scaling) $J_{k,\tau}\in\mathrm H^1(\Gamma;\mc M_k)$ with poles supported on $\Gamma\cdot\tau$. Conversely, such cocycles span the ``parabolic cohomology.''
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	In fact, the poles of $J_{k,\tau}$ have the same shape as the poles of the earlier defiend $f_{k,\tau}$.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	By linearity, one can define $J_{k,D}$ for any divisor $D$ with real multiplication. If it has ``strong degree zero,'' then we find
	\[J_{k,D}(\gamma)(z)=\sum_{\substack{b\in\Gamma\\\tau\in\left|D\right|}}i\left([r,\gamma r],[b\ov\tau,b\tau]\right)\left(\frac{b\tau-b\ov\tau}{(z-b\tau)(z-b\ov\tau)}\right)^{k/2}.\]
	Here, $i\left([r,\gamma r],[b\ov\tau,b\tau]\right)$ is a signed intersection number of some geodesics (in $\mc H$!), where $r\in\QQ$ is arbitrary. Notably, these intersection numbers are necessary to make the sum converge.
\end{example}
One now again takes some repeated integration, and we can state a Gross--Zagier conjecture. In fact, one can conjecture something about the prime factorizations of the special values, coming from some intersection numbers on Shimura curves.

\section{January 31: Geometric and Arithmetic Siegel--Weil Formulae}
This talk was given by Yu Luo at Johns Hopkins University for the JHUnior number theory days.

\subsection{The Geometric Formula}
For motivation, we may be interested in how an elliptic curve can map into an abelian variety. To geometrize, we let $\mc A_g$ denote the moduli space of principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension $g$.
\begin{definition}[Noether--Lefschetz cycle]
	For each $m$, we define the functor $\op{NL}(m)$ as living over $\mc A_1\times\mc A_g$ and parameterizing tuples $(E,A,\lambda,h)$ such that $h\colon E\to A$ is some map, and $\lambda$ is the polarizatino of $A$, and $\deg h^*\mc L_\lambda=m$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	It turns out that $\op{NL}(1)\cong\mc A_1\times\mc A_{g-1}$.
\end{example}
These cycles are interesting but difficult. We will be interested in similar questions with a little additional structure, coming from unitary Shimura varieties.

For today, $F$ is an imaginary quadratic field, and we let $V$ be a Hermitian spcae over $F$ with signature $(n-1,1)$ (where $n\coloneqq\dim V$). We also fix some level structure $K\subseteq\op U(V)(\AA^\infty_\QQ)$.
\begin{definition}[unitary Shimura variety]
	Fix everything as above. We define $\mc M_{K}$ to parameterize the data
	\[(E,\iota_E,A,\iota,\lambda,\eta),\]
	where $(E,\iota_E)$ is an elliptic curve with CM by $F$, $(A,\lambda)$ is a principally polarized abelian variety, $\iota$ is an $F$-structure on $A$ so that $\op{Lie}A$ has signature $(n-1,1)$, and $\eta$ is $K$-conjugacy class of a morphism $\widehat V(E)\to\widehat V(A)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example} \label{ex:unitary-sv}
	Choose a line $L\subseteq V$, and let $K$ be the stabilizer of a lattice $\widehat L$. Then $\mc M_K(\CC)$ consists of copies of symmetric spaces which look like $\op{Aut}(L)\backslash D$, where $D\subseteq\PP(V_\RR)$ consists of isotropic lines in $V_\RR$.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	There is a pairing structure on $\op{Hom}_F(E,A)$ by sending a pair $(x,y)$ to the composite
	\[E\stackrel x\to A\stackrel\lambda\to A^\lor\stackrel{y^\lor}\to E^\lor\cong E.\]
	In particular, this maps to $\op{End}(E)\cong F$, so we receive a pairing analogous to the Rosati involution.
\end{remark}
Having given our analogue of $\mc A_g$, we need to give an analogue of our special cycles.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a positive definite Hermitian $r\times r$ matrix $T$ with coefficients in $F$, and choose some $\mu\in K\backslash V(\AA_f)^r$. Then we let $Z(T,\mu)$ parameterize triples $(E,A,u)$ where $u\in\op{Hom}_F(E,A)^r$ satisfies $(u,u)=T$ (and some level structure dictated by $\mu$).
\end{definition}
It turns out that $Z(T,\mu)$ lives in $\op{CH}^r(\mc M_K)$, so we may take some linear combinations.
\begin{notation}
	For any $\varphi_f\in\mc S(V(\AA_f)^r)^K$, we may define
	\[Z(T,\varphi_f)\coloneqq\sum_\mu\varphi_f(\mu)[Z(T,\mu)].\]
	By definition of $S$, this is a finite sum.
\end{notation}
\begin{example}
	We continue from \Cref{ex:unitary-sv}. For $x\in L$, we define $D(x)$ to be the lines perpendicular to $x$. Then $Z(t,1_{\widehat L})$ on complex points is given by the projection
	\[\op{Aut}(L)\mathbin{\bigg\backslash}\bigsqcup_{(x,x)=t}D(x)\to\op{Aut}(L)\backslash D.\]
\end{example}
The geometric Siegel--Weil formula arises when $\op{rank}T=r-1$.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a positive definite Hermitian $r\times r$ matrix $T$ of rank $r-1$. Then
	\[\deg Z(T,\varphi_f)\sim E_T(\tau,\varphi),\]
	where $\sim$ means we are up to some explicit constant (coming from the Shimura variety), $\varphi$ is an automorphic form constructed with finite part $\varphi_f$, and $E_T$ refers to the $T$th Fourier coefficient for the Eisenstein series $E(\tau,\varphi)$ associated to $\varphi$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The Arithmetic Formula}
For the arithmetic Siegel--Weil formulae, we need to pass to some integral models. Accordingly, let $S$ be a finite set of primes containing $2$ and the ramified primes of the extension $F/\QQ$. For simplicity, we will also assume that the lattice $L$ of $V$ is self-dual away from $S$, and we assume that $K^S=\op{Stab}(\widehat L^S)$. In this situation, $\mc M_K$ admits an integral model over $\OO_F\left[S^{-1}\right]$. If we derive, we also receive the special cycles $Z^{\mathbb L}(T,\varphi_f)$.

In this case, we will be intersted in the case where $\op{rank}T=r$, which produces a cycle of codimension $1$ because $\mc M_K\to\Spec\OO_F\left[S^{-1}\right]$ has relative dimension $r$.
\begin{notation}
	We let $\op{Diff}(T,V)$ be the set of nonsplit primes $p$ for which $V_p\not\cong V(T)_p$, where $V(T)$ is the space with basis $\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$ satisfies $(e_i,e_j)=T_{ij}$.
\end{notation}
\begin{theorem}[Kudla--Rapoport]
	The special cycle $Z(T,\varphi_f)$ is nonempty only if $\op{Diff}(T,V)$ is a single prime $p\notin S$. In this case, the special cycle is supersingular over $\FF_{p^2}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	Suppose $\op{Diff}(T,V)=\{p\}$. Then
	\[\log\left(p^2\deg Z^{\mathbb L}(T,\varphi_f)\right)\sim E_T'(\tau,\varphi).\]
\end{theorem}
One can view this as a natural generalization of the Gross--Zagier formula to unitary Shimura varieties. The moral is that we are relating an intersection number to a modular form, which can then be related to a special value. Here are some ingredients of the proof.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item It turns out that
	\[Z(\tau,\varphi)=\sum_{m>0}Z(m,\varphi)q^m\]
	is a modular form. One can then take an arihetmic intersection with another special cycle to produce a genuine modular form. This produces a modular form whose coefficients are given by the left-hand side.
	\item On the other hand, we can use the right-hand side to produce a modular form. It turns out that the coefficients are the same at all but finitely many coefficients, but the difference is a modular form, so full equality follows.
\end{enumerate}

\section{January 31: Higher Siegel--Weil Formulae}

\subsection{Shtukas}
We are interested in function field analogues of the geometric and arithmetic Siegel--Weil formulae. The analogue of (unitary) Shimura varieties are given by shtukas. For our setup, we fix some \'etale double cover $X'\to X$ of smooth proper curves over $k\coloneqq\FF_q$; let $\sigma$ be the nontrivial automorphism.
\begin{definition}
	A \textit{$\op U_n$-bundle} is a pair $(\mc F,h)$ of a rank $n$ bundle $\mc F$ on $X'$ and an isomorphism $h\colon\mc F\to\sigma^*\mc F^\lor$. A \textit{Hecke modification} is a diagram $x\colon\mc F_0\to\mc F_1$, where $\mc F_0$ and $\mc F_1$ are $\op U_n$-bundles, $x\in X'$ is a point, and we are euipped with an isomorphism $\mc F_0|_{X'\setminus\{x,\sigma x\}}\cong\mc F_1|_{X'\setminus\{x,\sigma x\}}$. We also require there to be a natural short exact sequence
	\[0\to\mc F_0\to\mc F_1\to\delta_x\to0,\]
	where $\delta_x$ is the skyscraper.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $X'\to X$ is split, then a $\op U_n$-bundle is a vector bundle of rank $n$ on $X$.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}[shtuka]
	We define $\op{Sht}^r_{\op U_n}$ to be the moduli stack parameterizing diagrams
	\[\mc F_0\to\mc F_1\to\cdots\to\mc F_r\cong\mc F_0^\tau,\]
	where each $\mc F_\bullet$ is a $\op U_n$-bundle, each arrow is a Hecke modification, and $\mc F_0^\tau$ is the Frobenius pullback.
\end{definition}
Here are some grounding remarks about shtukas.
\begin{remark}
	There is a ``leg morphism'' $\op{Sht}^r_{\op U_n}\to(X')^r$ recording the points where the Hecke modifications occur. This is analogous to the structure morphism for a Shimura variety. It turns out that this map is smooth of relative dimensino $r(n-1)$. Thus, $\dim\op{Sht}^r_{\op U_n}=rn$.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	One has that $\op{Sht}^0_{\op U_n}=\op{Bun}_{\op U_n}k$, so functions on this space are basically unramified automorphic forms.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	For $n>1$, the space $\op{Sht}^r_{\op U_n}$ is not quasicompact.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	Taking the fibers over the various Hecke modifications, we produce ``tautological'' line bundles $\mc L_1,\ldots,\mc L_r$ on $\op{Sht}^r_{\op U_n}$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that $\op{Sht}^r_{\op U_n}$ vanishes for odd $r$, but it is not too hard to define a suitable analogue.
\end{remark}
We will also want some special cycles. The following definition is due to Feng--Yun--Zhang.

\subsection{The Formulae}
We will need to define some Eisenstein series.
\begin{definition}
	There is a Siegel Eisenstein series $\op{Eis}(g,s)_m$ for $\op U(m,m)$ such that each pair $(\mc E,a)$ (of rank $m$) has a Fourier coefficient $\op{Eis}_{(\mc E,a)}(s)_m$ with a rather technical definition. Our Eisenstein series are normalized so that the center of the functional equation is at $s=0$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	The Eisenstein series turn out to have explicit combinatorial expansions. For example, if $\coker a$ has length two, then $\op{Eis}_{(\mc E,a)}=q^s\pm q^{-2}$, where the $+$ sign is used if we are split over a split point $x\in X$.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}[Feng--Yun--Zhang]
	Fix everything as above, and take $m=n$ and a non-degenerate pair $(\mc E,a)$. Then for all $r\ge0$, we have
	\[\deg\left[Z^r_{\mc E}(a)\right]\sim\del_{s=0}^r\op{Eis}_{(\mc E,a)}(s)_m.\]
	Here, $\del_{s=0}^r$ means that we take $r$ derivatives and then evalaute at $0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix everything as above, and take $m=n-1$ and a non-degenerate pair $(\mc E,a)$. Then for every even $r\ge0$, we have
	\[\deg\left[Z^r_{\mc E}(a)\right]\cap[\mc L_1]\cap\cdots\cap[\mc L_r]\sim\del_{s=0}^r\left(q^{s\deg\omega_X}\op{Eis}_{(\mc E,a)}(s-1/2)_{n-1}L(\eta,2s)\right).\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	There is an analogous statement for $r\ge0$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	There is some analogous thing known for number fields when $r=1$.
\end{remark}

	is injective.
\end{definition}
There is a natural projection $\mc M(m,n)\to\mc A(m)$, and it turns out that there is a self-correspondence
\[\mc M(m,n)\from\mathrm{Hk}^1_{\mc M}(m,n)\to\mc M(m,n).\]
Thus, we can do cohomology to this correspondence, from which a Lefschetz trace formula for correspondences lets us compute $\deg\left[Z^r_{\mc E}(a)\right]$ as the trace of Frobenius acting via this correspondence. (Again, something similar happens for $m=n-1$.) We now discuss $m=n$ and $m=n-1$ separately.
\begin{itemize}
	\item Now, if $m=n$, there is a pullback square

\section{January 31: Hypergeometric Motives with CM}
This talk was given by Esme Rosen at Johns Hopkins University for the JHUnior number theory days.

\subsection{Hypergeometric Motives}
Our modular forms are classical ones. As usual, they have $L$-functions, which have analytic continuation, and they frequently have Euler products, and so on. We will be considering normalized Hecke eigenforms $f$, which also have associated Galois representations $\rho_f$, which are known to come from elliptic curves.
\begin{definition}[hypergeometric data]
	Fix $z\in\PP^1(\QQ)\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}$ and two lists of rational numbers denoted $(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ and $(b_0,\ldots,b_n)$ with $b_0=1$. Set $M$ to be their least common multiple. Then there is a hypergeometric motive defined over $\QQ(\zeta_M)$ related to classical hypergeometric functions
	\[_{n+1}F_n\begin{bmatrix}
		a_0 & \cdots & a_n & z \\
		b_0 & \cdots & b_n
	\end{bmatrix}=\sum\frac{(a_0)!\cdots(a_n)!}{(b_0)!\cdots(b_n)!}z^\bullet.\]
	The associated hypergeometric functions give rise to the periods of the motives.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	The data $((1/2,1/2),(1,1))$ gives rise to the Legendre family of elliptic curves $y^2=x(1-x)(1-zx)$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	The data $((1/2,1/2,1/2),(1,1,1))$ gives rise to a family of $K3$ surfaces, which are known to be modular at the CM points. These are symmetric powers of elliptic curves. One expects these to be associated to weight $3$ modular forms.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Let's construct the hypergeometric motives for $_2F_1$s; the general case is not too much harder. Start with the normalization $X_{\mathrm{HD}}$ of the curve
	\[Y^M=X^i(1-X)^j(1-zX)^k\]
	for some $i,j,k<M$ with $i+j+k\nmid M$. There is a standard $\gamma$ whose period is some quotient of $\Gamma$s along with a $_2F_1$ value. By diagonalizing the $\mu_M$ action on $X_{\mathrm{HD}}$, we can decompose the motive $X_{\mathrm{HD}}$. (For example, this certainly produces an absolute Hodge cycle.)
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	There are finite field versions of our hypergeometric series, basically obtained by replacing $\Gamma$s with some Gauss sums. Katz has shown that there are Galois representations whose traces are the correct hypergeometric sums over finite fields.
\end{remark}
Classical work in this de Rham realization has found that certain periods of modular forms (which are essential special values of the $L$-function) can be expressed as certain hypergeometric series. There is also work in the \'etale realization, expressing certain traces of Galois representations.

\subsection{Some Theorems}
Here is a main theorem.
\begin{theorem}
	There is explicit modularity for some hypergeometric motives of the type
	\[((1/2,1/2,r),(1,1,s)),\]
	where $(r,s)$ are some explicit pairs of rational numbers.
\end{theorem}
For another application, we consider Fermat motives. Fermat motives have complex multiplication, which means that their Galois representations are induced from Hecke characters. The periods of motives with CM by an imaginary quadratic field $\QQ(\sqrt{-D})$ are known by the Chowla--Selberg formula.
\begin{remark}
	Fermat motives arise in our story because they are $_2F_1(1)$ motives, which notably appear in the cohomology of Fermat curves. The associated Hecke characters are some Jacobi sums; we then take induction to get the associated modular form.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}
	For many of these hypergeometric motives, one can descend the Galois representations from $\QQ(\zeta_M)$ to $\QQ(\sqrt{-D})$ for some explicit $D$.
\end{theorem}
In fact, the theory of complex multiplication now allows us to compute some special values.

\section{January 31: Finiteness of Heights of Motives}
This talk was given by Alice Lin at Johns Hopkins University for the JHUnior number theory days.

\subsection{Our Statement}
We are going to be interested in heights today. Here is one such example.
\begin{theorem}[Faltings]
	Fix $g\ge1$, a number field $F$, and a finite set $S$ of finite places. Then there are finitely many isomorphism classes of $g$-dimensional abelian varieties $A$ over $F$ with good reduction outside $S$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
	The proof has two steps.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Show that there are only finitely many isogeny classes.
		\item Show that each isogeny class has only finitely many isomorphism classes. This is done basically by understanding how the Faltings height changes along isogenies.
		\qedhere
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}
The second step has been better understood recently.
\begin{theorem}[Kisin--Mocz]
	Assume the Mumford--Tate conjecture for an abelian variety $A$ over a number field $F$. Then for any $cf>0$, the set of abelian varieties $B$ over $\ov\QQ$ isogenous to $A$ (over $\ov\QQ$) with Faltings height at most $c$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Unlike in Faltings's situation, the set of abelian varieties $B$ (with unbounded Faltings height) is infinite.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	Recall that the Mumford--Tate conjecture asserts that the Mumford--Tate group agrees with the (connected component of the) $\ell$-adic monodromy group. Here, the Mumford--Tate group is the Tannakian algebraic group of $\mathrm H^1_{\mathrm B}(A(\CC);\QQ)$ in the category of Hodge structures, and the $\ell$-adic monodromy group is the Zariski closure of the Galois representation. The Mumford--Tate conjecture is wide open in the general case, but a lot is known.
\end{remark}
Here is our result, with definitions to follow.
\begin{theorem}
	Let $M$ be a Koshikawa motive defied over $F$, and assume the following.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The adelic Mumford--Tate conjecture holds for $M$.
		\item All Hodge classes are absolutely Hodge and compatible with \'etale--de Rham comparison maps.
	\end{itemize}
	Then for any $c>0$, the set of motives $M'$ equipped with an isogeny $M'\to M$ with height at most $c$ has only finitely many possible heights.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	The second hypothesis is true for abelian varieties, due to Deligne and Blasius.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	If $\op{MT}(M)$ acts irreducibly, then we can achieve only finitely many isomorphism classes.
\end{remark}
\begin{corollary}
	Fix a point $x$ on a Shimura variety $\op{Sh}(G,X)(F)$. Suppose that the Galois representation
	\[\rho_x\colon\op{Gal}(\ov F/F)\to G(\AA_f)\]
	has open image (so that the adelic Mumford--Tate conjecture holds). Then for any $c>0$, there are only finitely many $\ov F$-points in the Hecke orbit with height at most $c$.
\end{corollary}
Notably, this result is known due to Kisin--Mocz in the Hodge type case.

\subsection{Explanation of the Theorem}
Let's start by defining our motives.
\begin{definition}[Koshikawa motive]
	A \textit{Koshikawa (pure) motive} $M$ over a number field $F$ with weight $w$ is (roughly) a system of cohomological realizations, equipped with a $\widehat{\ZZ}$-integral Galois stable structure on the adelic \'etale realization. More precisely, we take
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A system of cohomological realizations: all the Betti cohomology realizations for each embedding $F\into\CC$, the de Rham cohomology, all the $\ell$-adic cohomology realizations, and
		\item A lattice: such as the $\widehat{\ZZ}$-coefficients \'etale cohomology.
	\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[isogeny]
	An isogeny $M'\to M$ of Koshikawa motives is a Galois-stable inclusion of the underlying integral lattices.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	This is analogous to the fact that an isogeny of abelian varieties amounts to the data of an embedding of the (adelic) Tate modules.
\end{remark}
We also need to define our height.
\begin{definition}[Kato--Koshikawa]
	Fix a Koshikawa motive $M$, and set
	\[V\coloneqq\bigotimes_{r\in\ZZ}{\det}_F\left(\op{gr}^rM_{\mathrm{dR}}\right)^{\otimes r}.\]
	Then integral $p$-adic Hodge theory provides an $\mathcal O_{F_v}$-lattice in $V\otimes_FF_v$ arising from the $\widehat{\ZZ}$-lattice; these assemble into an $\mathcal O_F$-lattice $\mc L\subseteq V$. Then we define the height
	\[h(M)=\frac1{[F:\QQ]}\left(\log\#\left(\frac{\mc L}{\OO_F\alpha}\right)-\sum_{\sigma\colon F\into\CC}\log\norm\alpha_\sigma\right).\]
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	When $M=\mathrm H^1(A)$, we find $V=\det\mathrm H^0(A;\Omega^1)=\mathrm H^0(A;\Omega^g_{A/F})$. The integral $p$-adic Hodge theory can be replaced with the N\'eron model $\mc A$ over $\OO_F$, allowing us to take $\mc L=\mathrm H^0(\mc A;\Omega^g_{\mc A/\OO_F})$. The height is then exactly the Faltings height.
\end{example}
Roughly speaking, the proof boils down to an isogeny formula for how our height changes in isogenies. The Mumford--Tate conjecture (and other inputs) are used in order to move the integral $p$-adic Hodge theory out into more controlled cohomology theories.

\section{February 3: Fulton--MacPherson Compactifications}
This talk was given by Denis Nesterov for the Harvard--MIT algebraic geometry seminar.

\subsection{Compactifying Configuration Space}
We work over $\CC$ throughout.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a smooth projective variety $X$ over $\CC$. Define the \textit{conjiguration space} $\op{Conf}_n(X)$ to be the collection of $n$-tuples $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ of distinct points in $X$.
\end{notation}
There are various compactifications of $\op{Conf}_n(X)$.
\begin{example}
	The projective scheme $X^n$ is a compactification of $\op{Conf}_n(X)$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	There is another compactification of $\op{Conf}_n(X)$ using Fulton--MacPherson compactifications $\op{FM}_n(X)$. Here, $\op{FM}_n(X)$ is the space of $n$ distinct points on the Fulton--MacPherson degenerations. These degenerations amount to adding in extra $\PP^{\dim X}$s to $X$, sometimes referred to as ``bubbles.''
\end{example}
One frequently takes a quotient $\op{Conf}_n(X)$ by the natural action by the symmetric group $\Sigma_n$. Then we can use Hilbert schemes to compactify.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a smooth projective variety $X$ over $\CC$. Then $\op{Hilb}_n(X)$ is the space of zero-dimensional subschemes $Z\subseteq X$ of length $n$.
\end{notation}
These compactifications have various relations. Here are some well-known ones.
\begin{definition}
	There is a \textit{Hilbert--Chow map} $\op{Hilb}_n(X)\to X^n/\Sigma_n$ given by sending a closed subscheme $Z\subseteq X$ to its support.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	If $\dim X=2$, then it turns out that $\op{Hilb}_n(X)\to X^n/\Sigma_n$ is a crepant resolution of singularities (i.e., it preserves the canonical bundle).
\end{remark}
\begin{conj} \label{conj:hilb-to-quot}
	If $\dim X=2$, then there is a ring isomorphism $\mathrm H^\bullet_q(\op{Hilb}_n(X))\cong\mathrm H^\bullet(X^n/\Sigma_n)$, where the former is a quantum adjustment of the ring structure taken at $q=-1$, and the latter is orbifold cohomology.
\end{conj}
\begin{proposition}
	There is an iterated blowup map $\op{FM}_n(X)\to X^n$. Explicitly, one blows up along the diagonals
	\[\Delta_k\coloneqq\{x_{i_1}=\cdots=x_{i_k}:i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_{k+1}\}\subseteq X^n.\]
	Explicitly, one blows up along $\Delta_n$, then $\Delta_{n-1}$, then $\Delta_{n-2}$, and so on.
\end{proposition}
\begin{remark}
	This proposition was the original definition of $\op{FM}_n(X)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Harder Relations}
Here are some harder relations.
\begin{theorem}[Haiman]
	Suppose $\dim X=2$. The ordered Hilbert schemes $\op{Hilb}_n^{\mathrm{iso}}(X)$ are isomorphic to $\op{Bl}_{\Delta^1}X^n$. Here, $\op{Hilb}_n^{\mathrm{iso}}(X)$ is the reduction of the fiber product sitting in the following pullback square.
	% https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsNCxbMCwwLCJcXG9we0hpbGJ9X25ee1xcbWF0aHJte2lzb319KFgpIl0sWzEsMCwiXFxvcHtIaWxifV9uKFgpIl0sWzAsMSwiWF5uIl0sWzEsMSwiW1hebi9cXFNpZ21hX25dIl0sWzAsMl0sWzIsM10sWzAsMV0sWzEsM11d&macro_url=https%3A%2F%2Fraw.githubusercontent.com%2FdFoiler%2Fnotes%2Fmaster%2Fnir.tex
	\[\begin{tikzcd}[cramped]
		{\op{Hilb}_n^{\mathrm{iso}}(X)} & {\op{Hilb}_n(X)} \\
		{X^n} & {[X^n/\Sigma_n]}
		\arrow[from=1-1, to=1-2]
		\arrow[from=1-1, to=2-1]
		\arrow[from=1-2, to=2-2]
		\arrow[from=2-1, to=2-2]
	\end{tikzcd}\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	This result allowed Haiman to realize MacDonald polynomials geometrically.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	This result was extended to $\dim X\ge3$ by Ekedahl--Skjelnes, where one has to take a large component of $\op{Hilb}_n(X)$ instead.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	By keeping track of the blow-ups, one can see that there is a map $\op{FM}_n(X)\to\op{Hilb}^{\mathrm{iso}}_n(X)$ when $\dim X=2$, which is a resolution of singularities.
\end{remark}
The existence of a map $\op{FM}_n(X)\to\op{Hilb}^{\mathrm{iso}}_n(X)$ tells us that we may gain access to \Cref{conj:hilb-to-quot} by taking cohomology. It turns out that
\[\mathrm H^\bullet(\mathrm{FM}_n(X))=\mathrm H^\bullet(X^n)[D_I],\]
where $D_I$ consists of the ponts living on some specified bubbles indexed by $I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$, and they satisfy some relations. One can also compute $\mathrm H^\bullet(\op{Hilb}_n(X))$ as being spanned by decorated partitions of $n$; this gives it an action of a Heisenberg algebra.

For our main theorem, recall that one uses various blow ups to produce intermediate spaces between $X^n$ and $\op{FM}_n(X)$. We can do something similar for the map $\op{FM}_n(X)/\Sigma_n\to\op{Hilb}_n(X)$.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a smooth zero-dimensional subscheme $Z$ of a Fulton--MacPherson degeneration $\widetilde X$ of $X$. For $\varepsilon>0$, we say that a pair $(\widetilde X,Z)$ is \textit{$\varepsilon$-weighted} if and only if we have
	\begin{listalph}
		\item $\op{mult}_x(Z)\le1/\varepsilon$ for all $x\in\widetilde X$,
		\item $\op{mult}(Z|_{\mathrm{bubble}})>1/\varepsilon$, and
		\item $\left|\op{Aut}(\widetilde X,Z)\right|<\infty$.
	\end{listalph}
	We now define $\op{Hilb}_n^\varepsilon(X)$ to be the moduli space of $\varepsilon$-weighted pairs $(\widetilde X,Z)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $\varepsilon=1$, then we are just looking at $n$ distinct points in $\widetilde X$.
\end{example}
\begin{theorem}
	The spaces $\op{Hilb}_n^\varepsilon(X)$ is a proper Deligne--Mumford stack.
\end{theorem}
\begin{example}
	With $\varepsilon=1$, we have $\op{Hilb}_n^\varepsilon(X)=\op{FM}_n(X)/\Sigma_n$. With $\varepsilon=1/n$, we have $\op{Hilb}_n^\varepsilon(X)=\op{Hilb}_n(X)$.
\end{example}
One expects that the map $\op{FM}_n(X)\to\op{Hilb}_n^{\mathrm{iso}}(X)$ suitably factors through $\op{Hilb}_n^\varepsilon(X)$. Here is the application: taking blow ups, one can interpolate between $X^n/\Sigma_n$ and $\op{FM}_n(X)/\Sigma_n$. By the main theorem, we know how to interpolate between $\op{FM}_n(X)/\Sigma_n$ and $\op{Hilb}_n(X)$. In this way, we are able to reduce \Cref{conj:hilb-to-quot} to an explicit calculaton on the surface $\CC^2$.

\subsection{Function Spaces}
Recall that our spectral expansion is
\[J_{\mathrm{spec}}(f)=\int_{\mf X(G)}D(\pi)\widehat\Theta_f(\pi)m(\ov\pi)\,d\pi.\]
We would like to explain what $\mf X(G)$ and $\widehat\Theta_f(\pi)$ are. In short, $\mf X(G)$ will be a space of virtual elliptic representations of $G(F)$, and $\widehat\Theta_f(\pi)$ is some weighted character. Namely, if $\pi$ is an induced representation of some $\sigma$ from a Levi subgroup $M\subseteq G$, then $\widehat\Theta_f(\pi)$ is the trace of an intertwiner constructed from $f$.

Let's start by fixing our nice functions.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a manifold $M$ and a vector space $E$ (of possibly infinite dimension). Then a function $f\colon M\to E$ is $C^\infty$ if and only if $\varphi f$ is smooth for every cotinuous $\varphi\colon E\to\RR$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
	Given a reductive Lie algebra $\mf g$, a smooth function $f$ on $\\mf g(F)$ is \textit{Schwartz} if and only if each $N\ge1$ and $u\in\op{Sym}\mf g(F)$ has
	\[\sup_{X\in\mf g(F)}\norm{X}_{\mf g}^N\left|\del(u)f(X)\right|<\infty.\]
	Approximately speaking, we are requiring all mixed partial derivatives to decay faster than any polynomial at $0$ and $\infty$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
	Choose a minimal parabolic $P$ and a maximal compact $K\subseteq G(F)$ so that we have an Iwasawa decomposition $G(F)=P(F)K$. Select $e_K$ in the parabolic induction $i_P^G(1)$ which is constantly $1$ on $K$. Then we define the pairing
	\[(f,f')\coloneqq\int_Kf(k)\overline{f'(k)}\,dk\]
	for $f,f'\in i_P^G(1)$. Then we define $\Xi^G\colon G\to\CC$ by $\Xi^G(g)\coloneqq(ge_K,e_K)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	The definition of $\Xi^G$ depends on many choices, but such choices only change the result by some scalars.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	The function $\Xi^G$ is supported on $K\backslash G/K=A^+$, and there is some machinery of matrix coefficients which tells us that it grows about as much as the square root of the modular character.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
	A function $f\in C^\infty(G(F))$ is \textit{Harish-Chandra--Schwartz} if and only if $P_d(L_uR_vf)$ is finite (where $L_u$ and $R_v$ are translations with $u,v\in U\mf g$), where
	\[P_d(f)\coloneqq\sup_{g\in G}\frac{f(g)\sigma(g)^d}{\Xi^G(g)}\]
	for any function $f$ and positive integer $d$. Here, $\sigma(g)$ is defined to be $\norm X$, where $g=k\exp(X)$ where $k\in K$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $G=\mathbb G_m$, then $\Xi^G$ is trivial (there is no parabolic anyway, and $K$ is a point), so $\sigma$ is just the logarithm. Upon identifying $\RR^+$ with $\RR$ by taking the logarithm, we recover just the usual Schwarz space.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	In practice, one can replace $\Xi^G$ by the $K\backslash G/K$ function which is $\delta^{1/2}$ on $A^+$. It is basically ``volume control'' because we want certain functions to be integrable on $A^+$ which may not otherwise look like it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Adjectives for Representations}
Here are some more adjectives.
\begin{definition}[tempered]
	A representation $\pi$ is \textit{tempered} if and only if $\left|(\pi(g)v,v')\right|\ll\Xi^G(g)$ for all $v$ and $v'$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It follows that the matrix coefficients all live in $L^{2+\varepsilon}(G/Z)$, for any $\varepsilon>0$. Intuitively, we are just putting some (volume-controlled) bound on the matrix coefficients.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}[square integrable]
	A representation $\pi$ is \textit{square integrable} if and only if $\pi$ is irreducible and the matrix coefficients for $g\in G(F)/A_G(F)$ are in $L^2$. The collection of such representations is denoted $\Pi_2(G)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Writing $P=MU$ and $K$ for a maximal compact, one has that $i_P^G\sigma\cong i_{K_P}^K(\sigma|_{K_p})$, where $K_p=K\cap P(F)$. Thus, twists by unramified characters should not adjust bounds on the space.
\end{remark}
\begin{notation}
	Let $\mc A_{G}^*$ ot $\mf a_G^*$ denote the (rational) characters from $G$. We let them act on representations with the additional scaling by
	\[\exp(\langle\lambda,H_G(g)\rangle),\]
	where $\langle\lambda,H_G(g)\rangle$ is the pairing extended linearly from being $\log\left|\lambda(g)\right|$ on genuine characters.
\end{notation}
Now, for fixed levi $M$, we choose two parabolics $P$ and $P'$ with Levi component $M$. For some representation $\sigma_\lambda$ of $M$, we can define an intertwiner $J_{P'|P}(\sigma_\lambda)\colon i_P^G(\sigma_\lambda)\to i_{P'}^G(\sigma_\lambda)$, which is normalized to $R_{P'|P}(\sigma)$. If one fixes a Levi $L\supseteq M$ and parabolic $Q\supseteq L$, one can use a technique called a $(G,M)$-family to produce an operator $R_L^Q(\sigma,P)$. We are now allowed to define
\[J^Q_L(\sigma,f)\coloneqq\tr\left(R^Q_L(\sigma,P)\circ \left(i^G_P(\sigma);f\right)\right).\]
These are our weighted characters.

\subsection{The Space \texorpdfstring{$\mf X(G)$}{ X(G)}}
Our next task is to define the space $\mf X(G)$.
\begin{notation}
	The set $\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$ consists of the isomorphism classes of the parabolic inductions $i_M^G(\sigma)$ for any Levi $M$ and $\sigma\in\Pi_2(M(F))$.
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that $i_M^G(\sigma)$ and $i_{M'}^G(\sigma')$ if there is a conjugation of the internal data.
\end{remark}
Thus, one can build some space $\widetilde{\mf X}_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$ which is a disjoint union of some orbis of representations $\sigma\in\Pi_2(M)$ over the Levi subgroups $M$ (considered up to conjugacy). One can give the space $\widetilde{\mf X}_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$ a topology; it is Euclidean if $F=\RR$ and a compact torus if $F$ is $p$-adic. As such, $\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$ is a quotient of this space and is thus realized as an orbifold. It admits a bundle $\mc E(G)$ whose sections over a given $\pi$ is the space of endomorphisms $\op{End}(\pi)^\infty$.

The space of sections $C^\infty(\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G);\mc E(G))$ admits a natural subspace $\mc C(\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G);\mc E(G))$ of ``Schwarz functions.'' We won't describe this subspace in the real case, but it amounts to the compactly supported functions in the $p$-adic case.
\begin{theorem}
	There is an isomorphism
	\[\mc C(G(F))\cong\mc C(\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}};\mc E(G))\]
	of topological vector spaces, given by sending $f\in\mc C(G(F))$ to the section $\pi\mapsto\pi(f)$. The inverse is given by sending a section $T$ to the function $g\mapsto\int_{\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)}\tr\left(\pi(g)^{-1}T_\pi\right)\mu(\pi)\,d\pi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	This is a strong Plancheral formula, in the sense that composing the two maps recovers the formula
	\[f(1)=\int_{\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)}\tr\pi(f)\mu(\pi)\,d\pi.\]
\end{remark}

\section{February 10: Bezrukavnikov's Equivalence}
This talk was given by Jialiang Zou at MIT for the categorical local Langlands seminar.

\subsection{Local Langlands}
Today, $F$ is nonarchimedean, $\OO_F$ is its ring of integers, $\varpi$ is a uniformizer, and $k$ is the residue field. We choose a split reductive group $G$ with Borel subgroup $B$ and torus $T$. We let $W_F$ be the local Weil group.

Our goal is to classify the irreducible complex representations of $G(F)$ by certain $L$-parameters.
\begin{definition}[Weil--Deligne]
	A \textit{Weil--Deligne} representation of $W_F$ in $\widehat G(\CC)$ is a pair $(\rho,e)$, where $\rho\colon W_F\to\widehat G(\CC)$ is a representation, and $e\in\op{Lie}\widehat G(\CC)$ is nilpotent, and
	\[\rho(w)e\rho(w)^{-1}=\left|w\right|e,\]
	where $\left|\cdot\right|$ is a suitable norm on $W_F$. An \textit{$L$-parameter} of $G$ is a Weil--Deligne representation $(\rho,e)$ where $\rho$ is semisimple.
\end{definition}
\begin{conj}[Local Langlands]
	Fix everything as above. Then there is a parameterization of $\op{Irr}_\CC G(F)$ by triples $(\rho,e,\chi)$, where $(\rho,e)$ is an $L$-parameter, and $\chi$ is an irreducible representation of the component group $\pi_0Z_{\widehat G}(\rho,e)$.
\end{conj}
\begin{remark}
	The data of $\chi$ is some kind of local system on the moduli space.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	One expects this parameterization to be a bijection if we add in the pure inner forms of $G$.
\end{remark}
A lot is known about this conjecture: it's known for some classes of representations, and it's known for classical groups. However, the general case is still open.

Let's prove a special case. Now, suppose that $G$ admits an integral model, so we may let $K=G(\OO_F)$ be a maximal compact, and let $I\subseteq F$ be the Iwahori subgroup, which is the preimage of the Borel $B(k)\subseteq G(k)$.
\begin{definition}[unramified]
	An irreducible representation $\pi$ is \textit{unramified} if and only if $\pi^K\ne0$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Unramified local Langlands]
	Fix everything as above. Then there is a parameterization of $\op{Irr}^{\mathrm{unr}}_\CC G(F)$ by triples $(\rho,1,1)$, where $(\rho,1)$ is an $L$-parameter, $\rho$ is trivial on the inertia subgroup $I_F$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Idea]
	The idea is that unramified irreducible representations are identified with simple module for the Hecke algebra $\mc H(G,K)\coloneqq C_c^\infty(K\backslash G/K)$. Then the Satake isomorphism can identify $\mc H(G,K)$ with $\CC[X_*(T)]^W$, which equivalently is $\CC[X^*(\widehat T)]^W$, which in turn parameterizes $\widehat G(\CC)$-representations, which can be turned back into semisimple elements.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
	One can parameterize the triple $(\rho,1,1)$ by merely understanding the image $\rho(\mathrm{Frob})\in\widehat G(\CC)$, which is a semisimple element considered up to conjugacy.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Iwahori Local Langlands}
Here is another special case.
\begin{definition}[Iwahori]
	An irreducible representation $\pi$ is \textit{Iwahori} if and only if $\pi^I\ne0$. This collection is $\op{Irr}_\CC^{\mathrm{IW}}(G(\CC))$.
\end{definition}
On the Galois side, we take tame ramification.
\begin{definition}[tamely ramified]
	A Weil--Deligne representation $(\rho,e)$ is \textit{tamely ramified with unipotent monodromy}, abbreviated TRUM if and only if $\rho|_{I_F}=\id$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Note that $(\rho,e)$ has allowed $e$ to be arbitrary! However, the data of $\rho$ still only amounts to the data of a semisimple element $s\coloneqq\rho(\mathrm{Frob})$ in $\widehat G(\CC)$. One can check that the condition $\rho(w)e\rho(w)^{-1}=\left|w\right|e$ is equivalent to having $ses^{-1}=qe$, meaning that we are looking at a pair $(s,e)$ of a semisimple element $s$ and a nilpotent element $e$ which commute up to the scalar $q$.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}[Iwahori local Langlands]
	Fix everything as above. Then there is a parameterization of $\op{Irr}^{\mathrm{IW}}_\CC G(F)$ by triples $(s,e,\chi)$, where $ses^{-1}=qe$ and $\chi$ is an irreducible representation of $\pi_0Z_{\widehat G}(s,e)$.
\end{theorem}
This parameterization is not expected to be bijective: one needs to put some restriction on $\chi$. The proof idea uses the Hecke algebra $C_c^\infty(I\backslash G/I)$.

Let's say something about the proof. On the automorphic side, it's worth noting that our representations are not too scary.
These representations are not too scary.
\begin{theorem}[Borel]
	An irreducible representation $\pi$ is Iwahori if and only if $\pi$ is a quotient of some unramified princpal series $I_B^G\chi$ (meaning that $\chi|_{T(\OO_F)}=1$).
\end{theorem}
Let's use this to prove the Iwahori local Langlands conjecture for $G=\op{SL}_2(F)$.
\begin{example}
	Take $G=\op{SL}_2(F)$ with $T=F^\times$, and our unramified character looks like $\left|\cdot\right|^s$ where $s\in\CC$. Then the induction $\pi(\chi)\coloneqq I_B^G(\chi)$ is unramified unless $\chi$ is $\left|\cdot\right|^{\pm1}$ or $\left|\cdot\right|^{\pi i/\log q}$. (The latter is a quadratic character.) Working with $\left|\cdot\right|$ discovers the Steinberg representation, and working with the quadratic character produces an unramified representation and a new ramified representation.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Let's classify $(\rho,e,\chi)$ for $\widehat G(\CC)=\op{PGL}_2(\CC)$. There are two choices for our nilpotent, up to conjugacy.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item With $e=0$, $s$ can be arbitrary, written $s=\op{diag}(t,1)$. Then $Z_{\widehat G}(s,e)$ is $\widehat G$ if $t=1$, it is $\widehat T\rtimes\ZZ/2\ZZ$ if $t=-1$ (because we can add in the Weyl group!), and it is $T$ otherwise. Thus, $\pi_0Z_{\widehat G}(s,e)$ is $1$ if $t\ne-1$ and is $\ZZ/2\ZZ$ if $t=-1$.
		\item With $e=\begin{bsmallmatrix}
			0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0
		\end{bsmallmatrix}$, then we see that $s$ must be $\op{diag}(\sqrt q,1/\sqrt q)$, and one finds that $Z_{\widehat G}(s,e)=1$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}
One can now match the above two classifications by hand.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The representation for $(s,0,1)$ is the unramified subquotient of $I_B^G\left|\cdot\right|^s$.
	\item The representation for $(\op{diag}(-1,1),0,\chi)$ (with $\chi$ nontrivial) is $\pi_\mu$, coming from the quadratic character.
	\item Lastly, the representation for the last parameter is Steinberg.
\end{itemize}
Let's now say something about the general case. One can identify Iwahori irreducible representations with the simple $H_I$-modules.
\begin{theorem}[Ginzburg]
	Simple $H_I$-modules can be identified with simple modules of $K^{\check G\times\CC^\lor}(\mathrm{St}_{\check G})$.
\end{theorem}
One can then embed simple modules $K^{\check G\times\CC^\lor}(\mathrm{St}_{\check G})$ into the enhanced Langlands parameters.

\subsection{The Steinberg Variety}
Of course, we are owed a definition of $\op{St}_G$.
\begin{definition}[Steinberg variety]
	Let $G$ be a reductive group over $\CC$. Then we let $\mc B$ be the space of Borel subgroups, and we let $\mc N\subseteq\mf g$ be the set of nilpotent elemets. Then we let the \textit{Steinberg variety} $\op{St}_G$ to be $\widetilde N\times_N\widetilde N$ to consist of triples $(e,b_1,b_2)$ whre $b_1,b_2\in B$ and $e$ is in the intersection $U_1\cap U_2$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Recall that there is a Springer resolution $\widetilde N\to\mc N$ given by the tautological bundle on $\mc B$.
\end{remark}
\begin{notation}
	For each $w\in W$, we let $\op{St}_{G,w}$ be the pre-image of the subset $Y_w$, where $\{Y_w\}_w$ is a suitable decompositoin of $\mc B\times\mc B$.
\end{notation}
The geometry of these Steinberg varieties are rather interesting.
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that $\mathrm{St}_G=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}\op{St}_{G,w}$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	One can check that $\op{St}_{G,w}$ is the cotangent bundle of $\mc B\times\mc B$ to $Y_w$. Thus, $\op{St}_{G,w}$ is a Lagrangian subspace of $\widetilde N\times\widetilde N$. Computing its closure is in general difficult.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	For $G=\op{SL}_2(\CC)$, one finds that $\mc B$ is $\PP^1$. The nilpotent cone $\mc N$ is two-dimensional. One can compute the Steinberg variety by hand. One finds that $Y_1$ is the closed subset of points $(e,e)$ (which is the diagonal), and the Steinberg has points $(e,e,e)$. Then $Y_2$ is the complement.
\end{example}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix everything as above. Then $\mathrm H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\mathrm{top}}(\mathrm{St}_G)$ is isomorphic to $\CC[W]$.
\end{theorem}
The moral is that we have a Springer correspondence sending irreducible representations $W$ to pairs $(e,\chi)$ where $e$ is nilpotent, and $\chi$ is an irreducible representation on $\pi_0Z_G(e)$. Namely, consider the homology $\mathrm H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\mathrm{top}}(\mc Be)$, where $\mc Be$ is the Springer fiber over $e$. Then note that $\mathrm H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\mathrm{top}}(\mc Be)$ admits an action by $Z_G(e)$, so one receives a decomposition
\[\mathrm H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\mathrm{top}}(\mc Be)=\bigoplus_{\chi\in\op{Irr}\pi_0Z_G(e)}E_\chi\otimes\chi,\]
and it turns out that this $E_\chi$ is an irreducible representation of $W$. The above theorem basically promises that varying over all $e$ will recover the irreducible representations of $W$.
\begin{example}
	For $G=\op{GL}_n$, one finds that $Z_G(e)$ is always trivial, so we are parameterizing irreducible representations of $S_n$ by conjugacy classes of nilpotent elements. But such conjugacy classes are classified by partitions!
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	Set $\mc B_e^s\coloneqq\{b\in\mc Be:sb=b\}$. If one restricts the Iwahori local Langlands correspondence to take $\chi$ appearing in $\mathrm H^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\mathrm{top}}(\mc B^s_e)$, then it is a bijection!
\end{remark}

\subsection{Bezrukavnikov's Equivalence}
We would like to build an analogue for the local Langlands correspondence which is less ``mixed characteristic.'' Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field, and $G$ is a reductive group over $k((t))$. One can, as before, define an Iwahori subgroup $I\subseteq G$.
\begin{itemize}
	\item On the automorphic side, there is the affine flag variety $\mathrm{Fl}\coloneqq G((t))/I$, and we let $D(I\backslash G((t))/I)$ to be the derived category of quasicoherent sheaves on $\op{Fl}$.
	\item On the spectral side, we consider the derived category of $\check G$-equivariant quasicoherent sheaves on the derived Steinberg variety $\mathrm{St}^L_{\check G}\coloneqq\widetilde N\times_{\mf g^\lor}^L\widetilde N$.
\end{itemize}
One statement of the equivalence is that the two categories defined above are equivalent. One can then say something about irreducible representations by passing to Grothendieck groups.

\section{February 10: Euler Systems and Relative Cohomology}

\subsection{Euler Systems}
Let's start with some motivation on Euler systems.
\begin{theorem}[Dirichlet class number]
	Fix a prime $q$ such that $q\equiv1\pmod4$, set $F\coloneqq\QQ(\sqrt q)$, and let $\chi$ be the corresponding quadratic Galois character. Then
	\[L(1,\chi)=\frac{h_F\log\varepsilon_F}{\sqrt q},\]
	where $\varepsilon_F$ is the fundamental unit.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[Cyclotomic class number]
	Fix a prime $q$ such that $q\equiv1\pmod4$, set $F\coloneqq\QQ(\sqrt q)$, and let $\chi$ be the quadratic character$\pmod q$. Then
	\[L(1,\chi)=-\frac{\tau(\chi)}q\sum_{a=1}^{q-1}\chi(a)\log\left|1-\zeta_q^a\right|.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Taking $\chi$ to be the quadratic character gives a formula for an interesting unit in $\OO_{\QQ(\sqrt q)}$. It turns out to generate a subgroup of index $2h_F$.
\end{remark}
For this talk, the goal will be to take an explicit special value formula (like the second theorem), an algebraic result (like the remark), and then we hope to prove a special value result like the analytic class number formula. There is a similar story for elliptic curves, and it is the general story for Euler systems, which provide the algebraic input for when we already have an explicit formula for an $L$-value (e.g., the Gross--Zagier formula).

Let's be more explicit about what our Euler systems are.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a nonarchimedean local field $K$ over $\QQ_p$, let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $K$ which admits an action by $G_\QQ^S$, which is the absolute Galois group unramified away from a finite set of places $S$. Further, we choose a Galois-stable lattice $T\subseteq V$.
\end{notation}
\begin{example}
	One can take $V=\QQ_p(\chi)$ with $T=\ZZ_p(\chi)$ for some character $\chi$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	One can take $T$ to be the Tate module of an elliptic curve.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}[Euler system]
	Fix a colletion $\mc L$ of primes, and let $\mc N$ be the collectin of products from $\mc L$ which are squarefree away from some prime $p$. Then an \textit{Euler system} is a collection of classes $\{c_m\}_{m\in\mc N}$ where $c_m\in\mathrm H^1(\QQ[m];T)$, satisfying some corestriction conditions
	\[\op{cor}_{\QQ[\ell m]/\QQ[m]}c_{\ell m}=\begin{cases}
		P_\ell\left(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell^{-1}\right) & \text{if }\ell\ne p, \\
		c_m & \text{if }\ell=p\text{ and }p\mid n,
	\end{cases}\]
	where $P_\ell$ is the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius acting on $V^*$ (hence the term ``Euler system'').
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Kolyvagin's approach requires producing ramified classes. This is difficult in general, so here the idea is to find ramified classes in highly ramified fields $\QQ[m]$.
\end{remark}
Kolyvagin then shows that $\mc L$ and $\im\rho$ being big (for Chebotarev reasons) allows one to bound the size of certain Selmer groups, of the form
\[\#\mathrm H^1_p(\QQ;T^*\otimes\QQ_p/\ZZ_p)\le\#\left(\frac{\mathrm H^1(\QQ;T)}{c_1\OO}\right)\]
for the choice of class $c_1$. If one takes a Bloch--Kato logarithm of $c_1$, then one frequently finds a special value of a $p$-adic $L$-function.

\subsection{Where to Find Them}
We are interested in finding more Euler systems. For our purposes, recall that $\mathrm H^1(F;V)$ classifies extensions
\[0\to V\to\mc E\to K\to0.\]
For our applications, $V$ comes from algebraic geometry, and $K$ has weight zero, so $\mc E$ is mixed and will need to come from somewhere interesting. Here is our idea: choose a subvariety $Y\subseteq X$ of codimension $1$. Then we have a composite
\[\mathrm H^{d-1}(Y;\QQ_p)\to\mathrm H^d((X,Y);\QQ_p)\to\mathrm H^d(X;\QQ_p).\]
We hope that $V$ can be found as a quotient of $\mathrm H^{d-1}(Y;\QQ_p)$, and $\QQ_p$ can be found inside $\mathrm H^d(X;\QQ_p)$ (possibly up to twisting), meaning that we can find our extensions from $\mathrm H^d((X,Y);\QQ_p)$. These will be behaved because frequently $\mathrm H^d(X;\QQ_p)$ is often associated to nice automorphic forms (where $X$ is a Shimura variety), so periods appear.
\begin{remark}
	Intuitively, we are replacing previous constructions of Euler systems (found from units in $K$-groups) with something more motivic (e.g., Nori's construction of mixed motives from relative cohomology).
\end{remark}
Here are some examples.
\begin{example}
	We can take $X$ to be the modular curve minus the cusps at $0$, and $Y$ to be the cusps at $\infty$. Then classes in $\mathrm H^1(X;\QQ_p)$ can come from certian differences of Eisenstein series, and there is a quotient map $\mathrm H^0(Y;\QQ_p)\to V$ given by evaluation. This recovers the Euler system for abelian characters of $\QQ$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Replacing $Y$ with CM points in the previous example, we recover elliptic units.
\end{example}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a modular form $f$. Then there is an Euler system for the symmetric square of $f$ and an explicit value formula.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Rerun the argument for the embedding of Shimura varieties coming from $\mathrm{Sp}_2\times\mathrm{Sp}_2\to\mathrm{Sp}_4$. Then the idea is to use the doubling method to express $L(\op{Sym}^2f\otimes\chi,s)$ as an integral.
\end{proof}
This method can also recover Kato's Euler system, an Euler system for the triple product, and infinitely many more.

\subsection{Elliptic Representations}
This part of the talk was given by Mitch. As usual, we are working with a reductive group $G$ over a local field $F$.
\begin{definition}[regular]
	An element $g\in G$ is \textit{regular} if and only if its centralizer has minimal dimension, which is the dimension of a maximal torus.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[elliptic]
	A regular semisimple element $g\in G$ is \textit{ellitpic} if and only if its centralizer (modulo center) has no split part.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	Generic elements in $\op{SO}(2,\RR)\subseteq\op{SL}(2,\RR)$ are elliptic. For example, this shows that the collection of elliptic elements is a semi-algebraic set in $\op{SL}(2,\RR)$ of positive measure.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that an element $g\in G$ is elliptic if and only if it is contained in no proper parabolic.
\end{remark}
We now turn to representations. Recall the following theorem.
\begin{theorem}[Harish-Chandra]
	Fix a smooth representation $\pi$ of $G$. Then the character $\Theta_\pi$ (which is a priori only a distribution) is represented by a locally integrable function on the regular semisimple elements.
\end{theorem}
\begin{definition}[elliptic]
	An irreducible unitary representation $\pi$ of $G(F)$ is \textit{elliptic} if and only if $\pi$ is non-vanishing on the collection of elliptic elements.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	The Steinberg representation is elliptic.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	The character of a representation induced from a parabolic subgroup automatically has character vanishing on the elliptic elements.
\end{remark}
Let's use our new adjective to classify representations. The irreducible tempered representations of $G$ can always be found in a parabolic induction $I_P^G(\sigma)$, where $\sigma$ is induced from a Levi $M$. Above a fixed $\sigma$, one can break the tempered representations of $\Pi_\sigma$ into those which are finite and related to the representation theor of some finite group $\widetilde R_\sigma$, which is a central extension of some subquotient of the Weyl group $W(M)$. (In particular, it is a quotient of the subgroup of $W(M)$ stabilizing $\sigma$.)

Now, elements of $\widetilde R_\sigma$ are considered ``regular.'' Its characters which don't vanish on the regular elements correspond to elliptic elements. Now, given a triple $(M,\sigma,r)$ with $r\in\widetilde R_\sigma$, one can produce a virtual distribution character $\Theta$.

The space $\mf X_{\mathrm{ell}}(G)$ of triples where $r$ is regular parameterizes elliptic elements. Then $\mf X(G)$ can be constructed as the virtual space of representations idnuced from $\mf X_{\mathrm{ell}}(M)$ as $M$ varies over the Levi subgroups. In particular, $\mf X(G)$ is a subset of the free vector space on the tempered representations of $G$. The moral is that we have found a manifold which (roughly speaking) parameterizes representations.

\subsection{The Weyl Integral Formula}
Fix an additive character $\psi$ on a local field $F$, which induces a duality $F\to F^*$. Further, fix a bilinear form $B$ on $\mf g(F)$ (where $\mf g$ is the Lie algebra of $G$).
\begin{definition}[Fourier transform]
	Fix a function $f$ on $\mf g(F)$. Then we define the \textit{Fourier transform}
	\[\widehat f(X)\coloneqq\int_{\mf g(F)}\psi(Y)\psi(B(X,Y))\,dY.\]
\end{definition}
\begin{notation}
	Let $\Gamma(G)$ be the collection of semisimple conjugacy classes of $G$.
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	Let $\mc T(G)$ be the collection of maximal tori in $G$. There is a way to put measures on all of them so that for a function $f\in C_c(\Gamma(G))$, we have
	\[\int_{\Gamma(G)}f(x)\,dx=\sum_{T\in\mc T(G)}\frac1{\#W(G,T)}\int_{T(F)}f(t)\,dt.\]
	These measures on the tori should be chosen compatibly with the chosen Haar measure on $G$.
\end{remark}
In life, one wants to integrate on $G(F)$. The following decomposition is occasionally helpful.
\begin{theorem}[Weyl integral formula]
	One has
	\[\int_{G(F)}f(g)\,dg=\int_{\Gamma(G)}D^G(x)^{1/2}J_G(x,f)\,dx,\]
	where
	\[D^G(x)=\left|\det(1;\op{Ad}_x;\mf g/\mf g_x\right|,\]
	and
	\[J_G(x,f)=D^G(x)^{1/2}\int_{G_x\backslash G}f\left(g^{-1}xg\right)\,dg.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Similarly, one has
	\[\int_{\mf g(F)}f(X)\,dX=\int_{\Gamma(\mf g)}D^G(X)^{1/2}J_G(X,f)\,dX,\]
	where $D^G$ and $J_G$ are defined similarly.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that the functions $D^G$ are locally integrable, the functions $J_G(x,-)$ are tempered distributions, and the functions $J_G(-,f)$ are locally bounded.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}[Idea]
	This formula is basically proven by passing from an integral over $G$ to an integral over the covering $(G/T\times T)/W$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Orbital Integrals}
We are thus motivated to compute some orbitral integrals.
\begin{notation}
	For some conjgacy-stable subset $\OO$ in the nilpotent cone, we define
	\[J_\OO(f)\coloneqq\int_\OO f(X)\,dX.\]
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	For a scalar $\lambda\in F^\times$, define $f_\lambda(x)\coloneqq f\left(\lambda^{-1}x\right)$, we have
	\[J_\OO(f_\lambda)=\left|\lambda\right|^{-\dim\OO/2}J_\OO(f).\]
	Here, if $\OO$ is a regular element, then $\dim\OO$ is $\dim G-\dim T$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	Any $G$-invariant distribution on $\mf g(F)$ which is supported on the nilpotent cone and homogeneous in the sense of the previous remark, then it is a linear combination of some $J_\OO$s, where $\OO$ varies over orbits in the regular nilpotent cone. In the $p$-adic case, this is not so hard because the nilpotent cone has only a finite number of orbits.
\end{remark}
These orbital integrals also have some nice duality: the Fourier transform is represented by a smooth function!
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a nilpotent orbit $\OO$. Then
	\[J_\OO(\widehat f)=\int_{\mf g(F)}\widehat j_\OO(Y)f(Y)\,dY,\]
	where $\widehat j_\OO$ is some smooth function on $C^\infty(\mf g_{\mathrm{reg}})$. In fact, $\widehat j_\OO(\lambda X)=\left|\lambda\right|^{-\dim\OO/2}\widehat j_\OO(X)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Here is a global version of this statement: on $G$, we have
	\[J_G(X,\widehat f)=\int_{\mf g(F)}\widehat j(X,Y)f(Y)\,dY,\]
	where $\widehat j$ is some smooth locally integrable function on $\mf g_{\mathrm{reg}}\times\mf g_{\mathrm{reg}}$.
\end{remark}
For certain applications (e.g., to the trace formula), we need to further weight our orbital integrals.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a Levi subgroup $M$ of $G$, and let $K$ be a maximal compact subgroup of $M(F)$. For a choice of parabolic $P$ with Levi subgroup $M$, define $\mc A_M\coloneqq\op{Hom}(X^*(M),\RR)$, and then the theory of $(G,M)$ families produces a volume term $\nu_M(g)$, which is the volume of convex hull of $H_p(g)\in\mc A_M/\mc A_G$ (where $H_p$ is a certain function $H_P\colon G(F)\to\mc A_M$).
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	In fact, there is a more refined weight $\nu_L^Q(g)$, where $L$ is a Levi containing $M$, and $Q$ is a parabolic whse Levi constains $L$. One has
	\[J_M(x,f)=D^G(x)^{1/2}\int_{G_x\backslash G}\nu_M(g)f\left(g^{-1}xg\right)\,dg\]
	for any $x\in M$ which is regular in $G$, and the refined weight $\nu^L_Q$ allows us to define a weighted orbital integral $J^Q_L$. (It turns out that $J^G_G$ is the full orbital integral on the regular elements of $G$.)
\end{remark}
Now, our trace formula will not be defined for all functions. (Indeed, on a continuous family of representations, the trace is generally infinite.) To fix this, we need a cuspidality assumption.
\begin{definition}[strongly cuspidal]
	Fix a function $f$. For a parabolic $P=MU$, we define
	\[f^P(m)=\delta_P(m)^{1/2}\int_Uf(mu)\,du\]
	and
	\[\varphi^P(X)=\int_U\varphi(X+N)\,dN.\]
	Then $f$ is \textit{strongly cuspidal} if and only if $f^P=0$ and $\varphi^P=0$ for all parabolics $P$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It is not hard to see that many orbital integrals vanish if $f$ is strongly cuspidal, which will allow our trace formula to converge. Let's be more precise about this. On the spectral side, the ``leading terms'' in the trace all come from some continuous spectrum which are induced representations (the more induced, the higher the term is). We are interested in the ``constant term,'' which is some invariant distribution, and we will only be able to see it by getting all the induced pieces to vanish. Thus, we will want to plug in strongly cuspidal functions. (A similar story holds on the geometric side with our orbital integrals.)
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	For elements $x\in M$ which are regular in $G$, one has $J_L^Q(x,f)=0$ unless $L=M$ and $Q=G$. 
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	The weighted orbital integral $J_M^G(x,f)$ does not depend on $K$, and it vanishes unless $x$ is an elliptic element in $M$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Quasicharacters}
A number of the items ($\Theta_\pi$, $\widehat j$, and $\widehat j_\OO$) defined today turn out to have many properties in common. For this subsection, $F$ is $p$-adic.
\begin{definition}[quasicharacter]
	Fix a $G$-invariant open subset $\omega\subseteq\mf g(F)$. Then we define a \textit{quasicharacter} on $\omega$ to be a $G$-invariant function $\theta$ on the regular elements of $\omega$ satisfying the following: for all semisimple $x$ and open neighborhood $\omega_X$ of $X$ such that $\omega_X^G\subseteq\omega$, there are coefficients $c_\OO$ for which
	\[\theta(Y)=\sum_\OO c_{\OO}(X)\widehat j_\OO(Y),\]
	where $\OO$ varies over the nilpotent orbits of $\mf g_X$. In other words, $\theta$ is locally a linear combination of Fourier transforms of nilpotent orbital integrals.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	For example, on regular semisimple elements, we are required to locally look the Fourier transform of $\delta_0$, which is constant. Thus, we are smooth on the regular semisimple elements. Away from regular semisimple elements, we are allowed certain singularities.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	There is a similar definition in the Lie group case.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	There is a definition in the real case, which requires one to take into account some derivatives as well.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	We can see that $\widehat j_\OO$ is a quasicharacter for each nilpotent orbit $\OO$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	For irreducible admissible representations $\pi$, it turns out that $\Theta_\pi$ is a quasicharacter.
\end{example}

\subsection{Parabolic Induction on Unitary Groups}
Here is a lemma to get us started. Let $(G,H,\xi)$ be a GGP triple; for example, if $H$ lives in codimension $1$, then $\xi$ is trivial.
\begin{lemma}
	Let $\pi$ be in $\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$, which means that it is the parabolic induction of some $\sigma$ which is from $L^2$. For any $S,T\in\op{End}(\pi)^\infty$, one has $SL_\pi T$ is smooth, and
	\[\mc L_\pi(S)\mc L_\pi(T)=\mc L_\pi(SL_\pi T).\]
\end{lemma}
Here, $\mc L_\pi$ is some interntwiner built from matrix coefficients of $\pi$, and $L_\pi\colon\pi^\infty\to\overline{\pi^{-\infty}}$ sends a vector $e$ to $\overline{\mc L_\pi(e,-)}$.
\begin{proof}
	We won't say anything about $SL_\pi T$ being smooth. Now, $L_\pi S$ and $L_\pi T$ are endomorphisms of $\overline{\pi^{-\infty}}$, and they have image in $\op{Hom}_H(\overline{\pi^{-\infty}},\xi)$. This space has dimension at most $1$ if $\pi$ is irreducible, in which case one can calculate
	\begin{align*}
		\mc L_\pi(SL_\pi T) &= \tr L_\pi SL_\pi T \\
		&= \tr(L_\pi S)\tr(L_\pi T) \\
		&= \mc L_\pi(S)\mc L_\pi(T).
	\end{align*}
	Now, irreducibles are dense in $\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$, and both sides are continuous in $\pi$, so we are done!
\end{proof}
We now want to understand how $\mc L_\pi$ ineracts with induction. Suppose that $P\subseteq\operatorname U(V)$ is the stable of some flag of (totally) isotropic subspaces
\[0=X_0\subsetneq X_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq X_k\subsetneq V.\]
In this situation, the opposite parabolic is the stabilizer of the opposite flag $\{\widetilde X_i\}$, where $X_i\oplus\widetilde X_i$ is always a non-degenerate hyperbolic quadratic space. Then the intersection of $P$ with its opposite is the Levi subgroup.

Observe that we may extend our isotopic flag to some flag
\[0=X_0\subseteq X_1\subsetneq\cdots\subseteq X_{k-1}\subsetneq X_k\subsetneq X_{k-1}^\perp\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq X_1^\perp\subsetneq X_0^\perp=V.\]
A choice of opposite flag allows us to split these quotients as follows, which one can check by chasing around the splittings of the quadratic spaces.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The quotients $X_i/X_{i-1}$ are given by $X_i'\coloneqq X_i\cap\widetilde X_{i-1}^\perp$.
	\item The quotients $X^\perp_{i-1}/X^\perp_i$ are given by $X_{-i}'\coloneqq\widetilde X_i\cap X_{i-1}^\perp$.
	\item Lastly, the quotient $X_k^\perp/X_k$ is given by $X'_{k+1}\coloneqq X_k^\perp\cap\widetilde X_k^\perp$.
\end{itemize}
Notably, we see that the spaces $X_i'$ and $X_{-i}'$ are all totally isotropic, and by construction, $X_i'\oplus X_{-i}'$ are mutually orthogonal. It turns out that the Levi subgroup is thus exactly given by
\[\op{GL}_E(X_1')\times\cdots\times\op{GL}_E(X_k')\times\op U(X_{k+1}').\]
For example, we see that $\op U(V)$ is quasi-split if and only if it contains a Borel, which means that there is a toral Levi subgroup, which then corresponds to being able to find a maximal isotropic flag $\{X_i\}_{i=1}^{\floor{\dim V/2}}$.

Now, take $G\coloneqq\op U(W)\times\op U(V)$, and let $Q\coloneqq Q_W\times W_V$ be some parabolic with Levi subgroups $L_W$ and $L_V$, which we expand out as
\[L_W=\op{GL}_E(X_1)\times\cdots\times\op{GL}_E(X_a)\times\op U(\widetilde W)\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_V=\op{GL}_E(Y_1)\times\cdots\times\op{GL}_E(Y_b)\times\op U(\widetilde V).\]
\begin{lemma}
	If $W$ and $V$ are compatible Hermitian spaces (meaning that $W$ includes into $V$, and the other direct summand is an odd-dimensional space $Z$ where $\op U(Z)$ is quasi-split), and $W$ can be decomposed as a sum of $\widetilde W$ and some hyperbolic space, then $\widetilde W$ and $V$ are compatible.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	One applies Witt's cancellation theorem.
\end{proof}
Applied to the above situation, one finds that $\widetilde W$ and $\widetilde V$ are compatible, so one gets a smaller GGP triple from these Hermitian spaces.
\begin{proposition} \label{prop:l-nonzero-along-induction}
	Let $\sigma$ be a tempered representation of $L(F)$ of the form $\sigma_{\op{GL}}\boxtimes\widetilde\sigma$, where $\sigma_{\op{GL}}$ is a $\boxtimes$-product of irreducible tempered representations of $\op{GL}_n$ factors, and $\widetilde\sigma$ is tempered. Letting $\pi$ be the parabolic induction, one has that $\mc L_\pi$ is nonzero if and only if $\mc L_{\widetilde\sigma}$ is nonzero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	By induction on stages, one can reduce to the case where $a,b\le1$. By further introducing new GGP triples, one reduces to the case $a=0$ and $b=1$ and $W$ has codimension $1$ in $V$. In this case, the operator $\mc L_\pi$ is absolutely convergent, and one can check everything.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Main Result}
Let's now sketch the following theorem.
\begin{theorem}
	If $m(\pi_0)$ is nonzero, then $\mc L_{\pi_0}\ne0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Because $m(\pi_0)$ is nonzero, there is a nonzero intertwiner $\ell$ in $\op{Hom}_H(\pi_0^\infty,\xi)$. Now, for any $e\in\pi_0^\infty$ and function $f$, we see that
	\begin{align*}
		\ell(\pi_0(f)e) &= \int_{G(F)}f(g)\ell(\pi_0(g)e)\,dg,
	\end{align*}
	which can be rearranged into
	\[\int_{H(F)\backslash G(F)}\ell(\pi(g)e)\int_{H(F)}(R(g)f)(h)\xi(h)\,dh\,dg.\]
	If $f$ is a Paley--Weiner function for $G$, meaning that the map $\pi\mapsto\pi(f)$ is smooth and compactly supported, then the inner integral is
	\[\int_{\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)}\mc L_\pi(\pi(R(g)f))\mu(\pi)\,d\pi\]
	by the Plancheral theorem. Note that $\pi(R(g)f)=\pi(f)\pi\left(g^{-1}\right)$, so we see that
	\[\ell(\pi_0(f)e)=\int_{H(F)\backslash G(F)}\ell(\pi_0(g)e)\int_{\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)}\mc L_\pi\left(\pi(f)\pi\left(g^{-1}\right)\right)\mu(\pi)\,d\pi\,dg.\]
	We now apply various test functions $f$. For example, if $T$ is a smooth and compactly supported section of the bundle $\mc E(G)$ of smooth endomorphisms over $\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)$, then we could define $f$ to have $\pi(f)=T_\pi$ by some argument with Fourier transforms. Then
	\begin{equation}
		\ell(T_{\pi_0}e)=\int_{H(F)\backslash G(F)}\ell(\pi_0(g)e)\int_{\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(G)}\mc L_\pi\left(T_\pi\pi\left(g^{-1}\right)\right)\mu(\pi)\,d\pi\,dg \label{eq:key-mult-eq}
	\end{equation}
	Now, $\pi_0$ is found in some induction $\op{Ind}_Q^G\sigma$, where $Q$ has Levi quotient $L$. We now choose $e\in\pi_0^\infty$ so that $\ell(e)\ne0$, and we may choose our section $T^0$ so that $T^0_{\op{Ind}^G_Q\sigma}e=e$ (in particular, $T^0_{\pi_0}e=e$ as well) and has support contained in the component $\{\op{Ind}_Q^G\sigma_\lambda:\lambda\in i\mf a_L^*\}$ of $\sigma$. In this situation, the above equation tells us that there is some $\lambda$ for which $\mc L_{\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma_\lambda)}\ne0$.

	We now decompose $\sigma$ as $\sigma_{\op{GL}}\boxtimes\widetilde\sigma$ and $\sigma_\lambda=\sigma_{\mathrm{GL},\lambda}\boxtimes\widetilde\sigma$. Thus, by \Cref{prop:l-nonzero-along-induction}, we see that $\mc L_{\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma)}\ne0$. This is almost $\mc L_{\pi_0}$! To finish, choose our section $T^1$ so that
	\[\mc L_{\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma)}\left(T^1_{\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma)}\right)\ne0.\]
	Then we define a section $T^2$ to have $T^2_\pi\coloneqq\mc L_\pi\left(T^1_\pi\right)T^0_\pi$. Now plugging in $T^2$ to \eqref{eq:key-mult-eq}, we see that $\ell\left(T^2_{\pi_0}e\right)$ is nonzero (because this is the same thing as for $\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma)$, so we can reapply \eqref{eq:key-mult-eq} with the representation $\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma)$ replacing $\pi_0$) and equals
	\[\mc L_{\pi_0}\left(T^1_{\pi_0}\right)\ell\left(T^0_{\pi_0}e\right)=\int_{H(F)\backslash G(F)}\ell(\pi_0(g)e)\int_{\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(Q)}\mc L_{\pi}\left(T_\pi^1\right)\mc L_\pi\left(T_\pi^0\pi\left(g^{-1}\right)\right)\mu(\pi)\,d\pi\,dg,\]
	where we have passed to $\mf X_{\mathrm{temp}}(Q)$ due to the support of $T^0$. But now the inner integrand is
	\[\mc L_\pi\left(T_\pi^1L_\pi T_\pi^0\pi\left(g^{-1}\right)\right),\]
	which was shown earlier. Calling this $\mc L_\pi\left(T^3_{\pi_0}e\right)$, we can go backwards and say that this is $\ell\left(T^3\pi_0e\right)$. But $T^3_\pi$ has $L_\pi$ as a factor, so it is nonzero, so $\mc L_{\pi_0}$ is nonzero.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
	Suppose that $\pi=\op{Ind}_Q^G(\sigma)$, where $\sigma=\sigma_{\mathrm{GL}}\boxtimes\widetilde\sigma$. Then $m(\pi)=m(\widetilde\sigma)$.
\end{corollary}

\end{corollary}

\subsection{The Kazhdan--Lusztig Isomorphism}
This first part of the talk was given by Jialiang.
\begin{theorem}[Kazhdan--Lusztig]
	There is an isomorphism
	\[\mc H(G,I)\cong K^{\check G\times\CC^\times}(\mathrm{St}_{\check G}).\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Here, $\mc H(G,I)=C_c^\infty(I\backslash G/I)$ is the Iwahori Hecke algebra. It turns out to be a $q$-deformation of the affine Weyl group $X_*(T)\rtimes W$ and thus depends only on the root datum. Thus, there is a Bernstein presentation as follows: there are operators $\{T_w\}_{w\in W}$ satisfying some classical braid relations, there are generators $\theta_{\lambda}$ for $\lambda\in X^*(T)$ (which satisfy additivity relations), and there are commutativity relations
	\[T_{s\alpha}\theta_\lambda-\theta_{s(\lambda)}T_{s\alpha}=\left(v-v^{-1}\right)\frac{\theta_{s\alpha(\lambda)}-\theta_\lambda}{1-\theta_{-\alpha^\lor}}.\]
	Note that sending $v\to1$ recovers the extended Weyl group $X_*(T)\rtimes W$.

	The idea of the proof is to embed both sides as certain endomorphism algebras of polynomial rings.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item On one hand, $\mc H(G,I)$ acts on $\mc M\coloneqq\mc H_{\mathrm{ext}}\otimes_{\mc H_f}\det$, where $\det$ is a certain determinant representaion given by $T_w\mapsto\det w$. This $\mc M$ is alternatively isomorphic to $\CC\left[v,v^{-1}\right][X_*(T)]$.
		\item On the other hand, recall that $\op{St}_{\check G}=\widetilde N\times_N\widetilde N$, where $\widetilde N$ is the cotangent bundle of the flag variety $\check G/\check B$, which is the Springer resolution of $N$. Thus, our right-hand side admits a natural action on $K^{\check G\times\CC^\times}(\widetilde N)$, which by a Thom isomorphism is identified with $K^{\check G\times\CC^\times}(\check G/\check B)$, which by induction is $K^{\check B\times\CC^\times}(\mathrm{pt})$, which is then isomorphic to $\CC\left[v,v^{-1}\right][X^*(T^\lor)]$.
	\end{itemize}
	It remains to show that the two representations are injective and are the same. Local methods shows that the representations are faithful. One can show that the image of the first one is contained in the image of the second one by tracking the action of certain generators. This produces a map from the left to the right, and one checks that it is an isomorphism with a rank calculation.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
	Bezrukavnikov's equivalence is a certain categorification of this argument.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	One can show that $\mc M$ in the above proof is $C_c^\infty((U,\psi)\backslash G/I)$ as an Iwahori Hecke module.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Motivation}
Fix a local field $F$ with absolute Galois group $\Gamma_F$, and let $G$ be a quasi-split reductive group. We will also let $\check F$ denote the unramified closure.

Recall that the classical local Langlands conjectures wants to associate irreducible smooth representations of $G(F)$ and Langlands parameters $W_F\to {^LG}$, where $W_F$ is the Weil group. In general, this map is only expected to be surjective (after passing to certain families of inner forms of $G$). To make the map injective, we should enhance the Langlands parameter by attaching to $\varphi\colon W_F\to{^LG}$ an additional representation $\rho$ of $C_{\check G}(\varphi)$.

This suggests that there is something stacky going on the spectral side.
\begin{definition}
	We define the \textit{stack of Langlands parameters} $\op{Log}_{^LG,F}$ to consist of those Langlands parameters $\varphi\colon W_F\to{^LG}$ up to $\check G$-conjugacy.
\end{definition}
Thus, enhanced Langlands parameter forms are coherent sheaves on $\op{Loc}_{^LG,F}$.

Now that there is some geometry on the spectral side, we may hope for some geometry on the automorphic side. For example, one now expects there to be an embedding $\op{Rep}G(F)\to\op{Coh}(\mathrm{Loc}_{^LG,F})$.
\begin{conj}
	There is a reasonable equivalence
	\[\bigcup_{L\text{ inner form}}\op{Rep}G'(F)\cong\op{Coh}(\mathrm{Loc}_{^LG,F}),\]
	where the left-hand side varies over inner forms of Levi subgroups of $G$.
\end{conj}
\begin{example}
	Let's explain why we may want to include Levi subgroups: even for $G=\op{GL}_2$, the point on the right-hand side coming from a principal series will have huge centralizer, thereby producing something like $[\mathrm{pt}/\mathbb G_m]$ on the right-hand side. There is only one representation of $G$ here, but we need tons of coherent sheaves, which are found by taking other inner forms coming from the Levi subgroup (which is the torus).
\end{example}
Descending to $\pi_0$ ought to produce the required bijection.

\subsection{Isocrystals}
The issue with our conjecture is that it is highly unclear how to take a union of categories. To this end, we will consider sheaves on the stack of $G$-isocrystals.
\begin{definition}[isocrystal]
	A \textit{$G$-isocrystal} is a $G$-torsor $\mc E$ on $\op{Spec}\check F$ equipped with an isomorphism $\mc E\to\sigma^*\mc E$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $G=\op{GL}_n$, then we are looking at a vector space $V$ over $\check F$ together with a bijective, Frobenius semi-linear endomorphism $\varphi\colon V\to V$. (Here, Frobenius semi-linear means that $\varphi(\lambda v)=\sigma(\lambda)\varphi(v)$.) This is the classical notion of an isocrystal.
\end{example}
Let's review the classical notion of isocrystal. For example, there is a classification.
\begin{theorem}[Dieudonne--Manin]
	All isocrystals $V$ over $F$ are direct sums of simple isocrystals, and they are classified by their slope. Here, for each $\lambda=d/n$ with $\gcd(d,n)=1$, the simple isocrystal $V_\lambda$ is given by
	\[V_\lambda\coloneqq\check F^n,\]
	where $\varphi$ is defined by $\varphi(e_i)=e_{i+1}$ for $i<n$, and $\varphi(e_n)=\varpi^de_1$.
\end{theorem}
One can make some similar theorems for $G$-isocrystals, which we will say something about later.
\begin{proposition}[Kottwitz]
	The groupoid of $G$-isocrystals is the quotient of $G(\check F)$ by $G(\check F)$ with the adjoint action with $\op{Ad}_\sigma(g)(h)\coloneqq gh\sigma(g)^{-1}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
	All $G$-torsors on $\check F$ are trivial because $\check F$ has global dimension $1$. Upon choosing an isomorphism (which is well-defined up to the adjoint action), we then use $\varphi$ to produce an element of $G(\check F)$, and the result follows.
\end{proof}
\begin{definition}[Kottwitz set]
	The \textit{Kottwitz set} $\mathrm B(G)$ is $\pi_0$ of the quotient $[G(\check F)/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}G(\check F)]$. For each $b\in\mathrm B(G)$, we let $G_b(F)$ be its automorphism group
	\[G_b(F)\coloneqq\left\{g\in G(\check F):gb\sigma(g)^{-1}=b\right\}.\]
\end{definition}
While we're introducing definitions, here are some moduli spaces.
\begin{definition}[loop group]
	The \textit{loop group} $LG$ is the functor
	\[LG(R)\coloneqq\op{Hom}(D_R^\times,G),\]
	where $D_R^\times$ is the punctured disk. When $\op{char}F>0$, then $D_R^\times\coloneqq\Spec R((t))$; for example, $D_k^*\coloneqq\Spec\check F$. When $\op{char}F=0$, then we use the Witt vectors and have $D_R\coloneqq\Spec W_\OO(R)$ and $D_R^\times\coloneqq\Spec W_\OO(R)[1/\varpi]$. (Because we are using Witt vectors, we should only use perfect algebras $R$.)
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[moduli of isocrystals]
	The \textit{moduli stack of isocrystals} is $\op{Isoc}_G\coloneqq[LG/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}LG]$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	On a perfect algebra $R$, one finds that $\op{Isoc}_G(R)$ is the $G$-torsors $\mc E$ on $D_R^\times$ equipped with an isomorphism $\varphi\colon\mc E\to\sigma^*\mc E$, which trivializes \'etale-locally on $R$. The reason for the trivialization is that the quotient is taken with respect to the \'etale topology.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	If $K$ is an algebraically closed extension of the residue field, then
	\[\op{Isoc}_G(K)\cong[G(\check F)/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}G(\check F)].\]
	For example, with $G=\op{GL}_n$, one can use the Dieudonne--Manin classification. The main content is that the stabilizers on both sides remains the same, which may be surprising if $K$ is large. Roughly speaking, the idea is that even for large fields $K$ of positive characteristic, its Frobenius-fixed points continue to be $\FF_p$.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Kottwitz Map}
Let's return to trying to classify our $G$-isocrystals. In our above language, this amounts to trying to understand the set $\mathrm B(G)$.
\begin{theorem}[Kottwitz]
	There is a canonical embedding
	\[\mathrm B(G)\to X_*(T)_{+,\QQ}^{\Gamma_F}\times\pi_1(G)_F.\]
	The two coordinates are called the Newton point $\nu_\bullet$ and Kottwitz point $K_G(\bullet)$, respectively.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	There is a description of the image of this map, but it is complicated. Heuristically, the Newton point is like the slope, so there is some integrality condition present, as in the Dieudonne--Manin classification.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	Consider $G=\op{GL}_n$. Then an isocrystal looks like
	\[V=\bigoplus_iV_{d_i/r_i}^{\oplus m_i},\]
	where $\sum_im_ir_i=n$. Then we can form a Newton point to be the Newton polygon $(d_1/r_1,\ldots,d_1/r_1,\ldots)$, where $d_i/r_i$ is required to apepar $m_ir_i$ times. The Kottwitz point is just $\sum_im_id_i$, which is an integer.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	We can give a partial ordering on $\mathrm B(G)$ by saying that $b\le b'$ if and only if $\nu_b\le\nu_{b'}$ and $K_G(b)=K_G(b')$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	For each $b\in\mathrm B(G)$, one sees that $G_b$ is an algebraic group oer $F$. In fact, it is an extended pure inner form of the Levi subgroup $M=C_G(\nu_b)$.
\end{remark}
A partial ordering allows us to distinguish some elements.
\begin{definition}[basic]
	A point $b\in\mathrm B(G)$ is \textit{basic} if and only if $\nu_b$ is central, meaning that $G_b$ is an extended pure inner form of $G$. The locus of basic points is $\mathrm B(G)_{\mathrm{basic}}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that the elements $\mathrm B(G)_{\mathrm{basic}}$ are the minimal ones for the partial ordering.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	Consider $G=\op{GL}_n$ again. By examining the simples, we see that
	\[G_b=\prod_i\op{GL}_{m_i}(D_{d_i/r_i}),\]
	where $D_{d_i/r_i}=\op{Aut}V_{d_i/r_i}$ is a simple central division algebra of $F$; it turns out to be the one corresponding to $d_i/r_i\in\op{Br}F$ (note we may identify $\op{Br}F=\QQ/\ZZ$). Thus, basic points are exactly those where $V$ is isotypic.
\end{example}
A partial ordering also allows us to stratify $\op{Isoc}_G$ into $\op{Isoc}_{G,\le b}$. Here is the main theorem on isocrystals for our purposes.
\begin{theorem}
	Let $\op{Isoc}_{G,\le b}$ consist of those points below $b$.
	\begin{listalph}
		\item The topological space $\left|\op{Isoc}_G\right|$ is the partially ordered set $\mathrm B(G)$ equipped with the partial ordering.
		\item The map $\mathrm{Isoc}_{G,\le b}\to\mathrm{Isoc}_G$ is a perfectly finitely presented closed embedding.
		\item The map $\mathrm{Isoc}_{G,b}\to\mathrm{Isoc}_{G,\le b}$ is an affine open embedding.
		\item The stratum $\op{Isoc}_{G,b}$ is the pro-\'etale classifying space of $G_b(F)$.
	\end{listalph}
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	This theorem tells us that $\op{Isoc}_G$ is indeed able to glue together its extended pure inner forms of its Levi subgroups. Indeed, (d) tells us that each individual groupoid $\op{Isoc}_{G,b}$ has coherent sheaves which are representations of $G_b(F)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Shtukas}
Let's say something about how our isocrystals relate to shtukas. The classical motivation is Deligne--Lusztig theory. Fix a reductive group $G$ over a finite field $k$. It turns out that $G$ is quasi-split by some version of Lang's theorem, so we may give it a Borel subgroup $B$. Deligne--Lusztig theory amounts to a long composite
\[B\backslash G/B\from[G/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}B]\stackrel{\mathrm{Nt}}\to[G/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}B]\cong\mathbb BG(k).\]
The first map is an isomorphism, the second map is the Newton map, and the last map is an identification. The moral is that there is push-pull map
\[\op{Ch}\colon\op{Shv}(B\backslash G/B)\to\op{Rep}G(k).\]
For example, each $w\in W$ produces an embedding $B\backslash BwB/B\to B\backslash G/B$. Pushing forward the constant sheaf on this point recovers the classical Deligne--Lusztig representations $R_w^*$.

We now repeat this idea for the affine case. Accordingly, we want to write down some composite
\[I\backslash LG/I\stackrel\delta\from[LG/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}I]\stackrel{\mathrm{Nt}}\to[LG/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}LG].\]
The middle object will be the moduli of local shtukas.
\begin{definition}[local Hecke stack]	
	The \textit{local Hecke stack} $\mathrm{Hk}$ is the quotient $I\backslash LG/I$, where $I$ is the Iwahori subgroup. Given an algebra $R$, $\mathrm{Hk}(R)$ parameterizes pairs of $I$-torsors $\mc E_1$ and $\mc E_2$ on $D_R$, equipped with an isomorphism $\varphi$ between them on the punctured disk $D_R^\times$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}[local shtuka]
	The \textit{local shtuka stack} is the quotient $[LG/_{\mathrm{Ad}_\sigma}I]$. Given an algebra $R$, $\op{Sht}(R)$ parameterizes $I$-torsors $\mc E$ on $D_R$ along with an isomorphism $\mc E|_{D_R^\times}\to\sigma^*\mc E|_{D_R^\times}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	The fibers of $\mathrm{Nt}$ look like $LG/I$, which is an affine flag variety. It follows that $\mathrm{Nt}$ is ``ind-proper.''
\end{remark}

\subsection{March 3: Gluing Triples}
This talk was given by Piotr Achinger at MIT for the number theory seminar.

\subsection{Motivation}
For motivation, given a prime $p$ and a variety $X$ over $\QQ_p$, we are interested in understanding the models $\mc X$ of $X$ over $\ZZ_p$. For our purposes, we are just hunting for schemes $\mc X$ with the correct generic fiber. Frequently, we will refine this question to require $\mc X$ to be flat or proper or similar, but we will not do so yet.
\begin{example}
	The theory of stable curves would like to understand degenerations of curves.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	The theory of N\'eron models finds models for abelian varieties.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	The theory of integral models of Shimura varieties is very rich.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	There is some Bruhat--Tits theory which is interested in finding models for algebraic groups.
\end{example}
We will approach this problem with nonarchimedean geometry. Here is why this may be a reasonable thing to do.
\begin{example}
	The scheme $\AA^1_{\QQ_p}$ of course admits an integral model as $\AA^1_{\ZZ_p}$, which amounts to a choice of some integral points $\ZZ_p\subseteq\QQ_p$, which is some analytic open subset. But different models roughly correspond to linear transforms of our integral model, which amount to a different choice of rigid analytic open disk.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	The integral standard integral model of the modular curves $Y(N)$ more or less corresponds to the points $\{E:\left|j(E)\right|\le1\}$.
\end{example}
Let's now describe the general setup for the rest of the talk. Le $S$ be a Noetherian scheme, $S_0\subseteq S$ is a closed subscheme cut out by some ideal sheaf $\mc I$, and we set $S_n\coloneqq V\left(\mc I^{n+1}\right)$ for each $n$. We let $\hat S$ be the formal completion, $S^\circ\coloneqq S\setminus S_0$ be the generic part, and $\hat S_{\mathrm{rig}}$ is the associated rigid analytic space which looks like $\hat S\setminus S_0$. Thus, we have a commutative diagram as follows.

We would like to have a similar theorem for schemes over $S$. Of course, schemes are themselves sheaves, but they are probably not coherent. One can try to build a more general version of this theorem, but it is not so easy.

\subsection{Where to Find Gluing Triples}
Continue with the setup of the previous subsection. Further, let $X$ be a scheme which is locally of finite type, let $X^\circ\coloneqq X_{S^\circ}$ be its generic fiber, let $\hat X$ be the formal completion. Then we have rigid spaces $(X^\circ)^{\mathrm{an}}$ which is the (rigid) analytification and $\hat X_{\mathrm{rig}}$ which is the rigid generic fiber.

One may hope that our ``gluing triple'' simply amounts to identifying the two rigid spaces, but this cannot be the case.
\begin{example}
	When $X=\AA^1_{\ZZ_p}$, one finds that $(X^\circ)^{\mathrm{an}}$ is the full analytification of $\AA^1$, but $\hat X_{\mathrm{rig}}$ is merely an open disk.
\end{example}
Nonetheless, there is a local isomorphism $j_X\colon\hat X_{\mathrm{rig}}\to(X^\circ)^{\mathrm{an}}$.
\begin{remark}
	One can check that $X$ being separated over $S$ is equivalent to $X^\circ\to S^\circ$ and $\hat X\to\hat S$ being separated; in this case, $j_X$ is an open embedding. If $X$ is further proper, then those two maps are proper, and $j_X$ is an isomorphism.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}[gluing triples]
	A \textit{(separated) gluing triple} on $(S,S_0)$ is a triple $(U,\mc X,\eta)$, where $U$ is a scheme locally of finite type and separated over $S^\circ$, a formal scheme $\mc X$ which is separated and locally of finite type over $\hat S$, and an open embedding $j\colon\mc X_{\mathrm{rig}}\to U^{\mathrm{an}}$.
\end{definition}
We would this like to understand when all gluing triples actually come from genuine schemes.
\begin{theorem}
	The functor $t\colon\op{Sch}_S^{\mathrm{sep},\mathrm{loc. fin. type}}\to\mathrm{Trp}$ (which the latter is the category of gluing triples) given by
	\[t(X)\coloneqq(X^\circ,\hat X,j_X)\]
	is fully faithful.
\end{theorem}
Of course, the interesting question is essential surjectivity. It turns out to not be essentially surjective. To fix it, we need algebraic spaces.
\begin{definition}[algebraic space]
	An \textit{algebraic space} over $S$ is an fppf sheaf $X\colon\mathrm{Sch}_S\to\mathrm{Set}$ admitting an \'etale surjection from a scheme. We further require that the diagonal $X\to X\times_SX$ is representable and quasicompact.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Algebraic spaces are occasionally more flexible to work with than schemes because there are Artin criteria to test if some fppf sheaf is an algebraic space.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	Choose a prime $p\ge7$. Then there is a smooth proper algebraic space $X$ over $\ZZ_p$ (which is not a scheme) such that $X_{\QQ_p}$ and $X_{\FF_p}$ are projective K3 surfaces.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	There is a K3 surface $X$ over $\QQ$ such that $X_{\QQ_p}$ has a smooth proper model $\mc X$ over $\ZZ_p$ but for which $X$ does not extend to a smooth proper scheme over $\ZZ_{(p)}$.
\end{example}
Thus, we see that our gluing triples may come from algebraic spaces instead of merely schemes!
\begin{theorem}
	The functor $t$ is an equivalence if one extends the source to separated algebraic spaces which are locally of finite type.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	There is even a notion of a coherent sheaf on a gluing triple, and one can show that $\op{Coh}(X)$ is equivalent to $\op{Coh}(t(X))$. In particular, $X$ is now uniquely determined from $t(X)$ because spaces can be reconstructed from their tensor category of coherent sheaves.
\end{remark}

\section{March 10: Sheaves on Isocrystals}
This talk was given by Zeyu at MIT for the categorical Local Langlands seminar.

\subsection{Some Category Theory}
Let $F$ be a $p$-adic local field, and we choose a reductive group $G$ over $F$. We let $\check F$ denote the completion of the unramified closure of $F$, and we choose a model $\mc G$ of $G$ over $\OO_F$. Last time, we defined $\mathrm B(G)=G(\check F)/\op{Ad}_\sigma G(\check F)$, and we defined loop groups $L^+G$ and $LG$, which allowed us to define a stack $\op{Isoc}_G$ of isocrystals as $LG/\op{Ad}_\sigma LG$.
\begin{theorem}
	For any algebraically closed extension $K$ of $\kappa_F$, there is a bijection $\op{Isoc}_G(K)\to\mathrm BG$.
\end{theorem}
Thus, the stratification of $\mathrm BG$ (using Newton and Kottwitz points) gives a stratification for $\op{Isoc}_G$.

Today, we would like to define a category of sheaves on $\op{Isoc}_G$. Unfortunately, we will have to use some $\infty$-category theory. Let $\op{Pr}^L$ be the $\infty$-category of presentable $\infty$-categories (with morphisms given by colimit-preserving functors), where presentable means that it admits all colimits (of some bounded cardinality). We also fix a coefficient field $\Lambda\in\{\overline\QQ_\ell,\QQ_\ell,\ZZ_\ell,\FF_\ell\}$, which allows us to define $\op{LinCat}_\Lambda$ to be the category of $\Lambda$-modules of $\op{Pr}^L$.
\begin{definition}[compact]
	An object $x$ in a category $\mc C$ is \textit{compact} if and only if the functor $\op{Hom}(x,-)$ is continuous. We let $\mc C^\omega$ denote the full subcategory of compact objects. The category $\mc C$ is \textit{compactly generated} if and only if any object in $\mc C$ is a filtered colimit of objects in $\mc C^\omega$.
\end{definition}
Here are some categorical constructions.
\begin{definition}
	Given a category $\mc C$, we define the category $\op{Ind}\mc C$ to consist of objects which are filtered systems $\{\mc F_i\}$ (denoted $\colim_i\mc F_i$) with morphisms given by
	\[\op{Hom}(\colim\mc F_i,\colim\mc G_j)=\lim_i\colim_j\op{Hom}(\mc F_i,\mc G_j)\]
\end{definition}
It is worthwhile to have some explicit constructions of limits and colimits in $\mathrm{LinCat}_\Lambda$.
\begin{definition}
	Given a collection of $\Lambda$-linear categories $\{\mc C_i\}$, we define the category $\lim\mc C_i$ as having objects which are infinite tuples $(c_i)_i$ such that $c_i\in\mc C_i$ and are equipped with identifications between the $c_i\in\mc C_i$ and $c_j\in\mc C_j$. The morphisms are defined by some limit of morphisms.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
	Given a collection of $\Lambda$-linear categories $\{\mc C_i\}$ for which the transition maps $L_{ij}\colon\mc C_i\to\mc C_j$ admit right adjoints (which is equivalent to $L_{ij}$ preserving compact objects), we may define $\colim\mc C_i$ as the corresponding limit of the right adjoints. 
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	If the $\mc C_i$s are compactly generated, then $\colim\mc C_i$ is also compactly generated. If the index se is filtered, then one has an explicit description of the morphisms.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Category of Sheaves}
We are now ready to define our category of sheaves. Here are sheaves on schemes.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a perfect finitely presented scheme $X$, we define $\op{Shv}X\coloneqq\op{Ind}\op{Shv}_cX$ to be the inductive limit of the (derived) category of constructible sheaves on $X$. For general quasicompact quasiseparated schemes $X$, we may write $X$ as a limit of perfect finitely presented schemes $\{X_i\}$, so we define
	\[\op{Shv}X\coloneqq\colim\op{Shv}X_i,\]
	where the internal maps are given by $(-)^!$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Because $(-)^!$ preserves compact objects, it admits a right adjoint $(-)_\flat$, which allows us to express the colimit category as a limit.
\end{remark}
And here are sheaves on stacks.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a prestack $X$, which can be expressed as a colimit of quasicompact quasiseparated schemes $\{X_i\}$. Then we define
	\[\op{Shv}X\coloneqq\lim\op{Shv}X_i,\]
	where the internal maps are again given by $(-)^!$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	Suppose that $K$ is a limit of finite groups $\{K_i\}$ so that $\op{Shv}K=\lim_i\op{Shv}K_i$. Let $p\colon K\to\mathrm{pt}$ be the canonical projection, which yields adjoint functors $\left(p^!,p_\flat\right)$. For example, there is an object $\omega_K\coloneqq p^!\Lambda$. We claim that $p_\flat\omega_K$ is $C^\infty(K)$. Indeed, we may compute
	\[\op{Hom}(\Lambda,p_\flat\omega_K)=\op{Hom}(\omega_K,\omega_K).\]
	This latter space can be expanded out in terms of the inductive limit, and eventually it unwinds correctly.
\end{example}

	We claim that $\op{Shv}\mathbb B_{\mathrm{pro\acute et}}K$ is $\op{Rep}^{\mathrm{sm}}K$. \subsection{Duality of Sheaves}
It turns out that $\op{Shv}(\mathrm{Isoc}_G)$ admits a duality, and explaining this is our next goal.
\begin{definition}[dualizable]
	A $\Lambda$-linear category $\mc C$ is \textit{dualizable} if and only if there is a category $\mc C^\lor$ which admits a unit $\mathrm{Mod}_\Lambda\to\mc C\otimes\mc C^\lor$ and a counit $\mc C\otimes\mc C^\lor\to\mathrm{Mod}_\Lambda$, satisfying various compatibilities. We let the pair of unit and counit be denoted $\mathbb D$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example} \label{ex:duality-for-schemes}
	Let $X$ be a pfp scheme, and set $\mc C\coloneqq\op{Shv}(X)$. Then there is a counit $\op{Shv}(X)\otimes\op{Shv}(X)\to\mathrm{Mod}_\Lambda$ given by $\Gamma(-\otimes^!-)$. To define the unit, we would like to take $\Delta_*\omega_X$, but this is a sheaf on $X\times X$, not in the tensor product. To fix this, we simply pass through the right adjoint $\boxtimes^R$ of $\boxtimes\colon\op{Shv}(X)\otimes\op{Shv}(X)\to\op{Shv}(X\times X)$.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	If $\mc C$ is compactly generated with dual category $\mc C^\lor$, then there is an equivalence
	\[\mathbb D^\omega\colon(\mc C^\omega)\opp\to(\mc C^\lor)^\omega\]
	satisfying $\op{Hom}(x,y)$ is equal to the counit of $\mathbb D^\omega(x)\otimes y$.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	Let's extend \Cref{ex:duality-for-schemes} to work with $\op{Isoc}_G$. The main issue is that there is not an obvious candidate for the functor $\Gamma$, which should be pushforward to a point. But this sort of pushforward requires an argument to exist. Well, we can simply define $\Gamma$ on the cover $\op{Shv}(\mathrm{Sht}^{\mathrm{loc}})$.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Selmer Subgroup}
Let's review what we did last time. Fix an (affine) algebraic group $G$ over a field $k$. Given a $G$-torsor $f\colon Z\to X$, note that any rational point $x\in X(K)$ produces a fiber $Z_x\to\{x\}$ living in the pullback square

\end{tikzcd}\]
which is then manifestly another $G$-torsor.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a $G$-torsor $f\colon Z\to X$. For $\tau\in\mathrm H^1(k;G)$, we define $X(k)^\tau$ to be the collection of rational points $x\in X(k)$ for which the class $[Z_x]$ agrees with the class of $\tau$.
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	We thus have a partition
	\[X(k)=\bigsqcup_{\tau\in\mathrm H^1(k;G)}X(k)^\tau.\]
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	By some twisting, we see that $Z_x=f^\tau(Z^\tau(k))$ because a torsor over $k$ is trivial if and only if it admits a rational point.
\end{remark}
We are interested in detecting when $X(k)^\tau$ may admit a rational point. For example, with $k$ being a number field, we see that $k\into\AA_k$ implies that $f^\tau(Z^\tau(k))\into f^\tau(Z^\tau(\AA_k))$, so the former being nonempty implies that the latter is nonempty.
\begin{definition}[Selmer set]
	Fix a $G$-torsor $f\colon Z\to X$. Then we define
	\[\op{Sel}_2(k;G)\coloneqq\left\{\tau\in\mathrm H^1(k;G):Z^\tau(\AA_k)\ne\emp\right\}.\]
\end{definition}
The point is that our hunt for rational points may only focus on $\op{Sel}_2(k;G)$.

Our next goal is to show that $\op{Sel}_2(k;G)$ is finite.
\begin{proposition} \label{prob:local-finite}
	Fix a finite \'etale group scheme $F$ over a local field $k$, and let $X$ be a proper variety over $k$. For any $F$-torsor $f\colon Z\to X$, the map
	\[X(k)\to\mathrm H^1(k;F)\]
	has finite image.
\end{proposition}
The key input to the proof if Krasner's lemma.
\begin{lemma} \label{lem:krasner}
	Let $f\colon Y\to X$ be a finite \'etale scheme over $k$. For each $x\in X(k)$, there is an open analytic neighborhood of $x\in U$ such that all the fibers $f^{-1}(\{x'\})\subseteq Y$ are isomorphic for $x'\in U$.
\end{lemma}
This is a geometric incarnation of the usual Krasner's lemma for roots of polynomials. We will omit the proof.
\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{prob:local-finite}]
	The main point is that $X(k)\to\mathrm H^1(k;F)$ is continuous, where the target has been given the discrete topology: indeed, we need to show that the subset of $x\in X(k)$ for which $Z_x$ has the same type in $\mathrm H^1(k;F)$ is an open subset. But this follows by a $G$-equivariant version of \Cref{lem:krasner}. (Alternatively, we can apply \Cref{lem:krasner} directly to the cover $G\times Z\to X$.) This continuity now completes the proof: $X$ is proper, so $X(k)$ is compact, so its image in the discrete topology $\mathrm H^1(klF)$ must be finite!
\end{proof}
And here is our theorem.
\begin{theorem}
	Let $f\colon Z\to X$ be a $G$-torsor, where $X$ is proper variety over a number field $k$. Then $\op{Sel}_2(k;G)$ is finite.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Having a torsor can be described by some equations and some open subset, so one can spread out the $G$-torsor $f\colon Z\to X$ to a $\mc G$-torsor $f\colon\mc Z\to\mc X$, where the latter objects all live over $\OO_{K,S}$ for some finite set of places $S$. Notably, by properness, we see that $X(k_v)=\mc X(\OO_v)$. Thus, we have a commutative diagram as follows.

\end{remark}

Here is our refinement.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a $G$-torsor $f\colon X\to Z$ over a number field $k$. Then we define
	\[X(\AA_k)^f\coloneqq\bigcup_{\tau\in\op{Sel}_2(k;G)}f^\tau(Z^\tau(\AA_f)).\]
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	By definition, we see that $X(\AA_k)^f$ is nonempty if and only if $\op{Sel}_2(k;G)$ is nonempty.
\end{remark}
Then we see that $X(k)$ embeds into $X(\AA_k)^f$, which consists of those $x\in X(\AA_k)$ which are in the image of
\[\mathrm H^1(k;G)\to\prod_v\mathrm H^1(k_v;G).\]
By letting $f$ vary over all $G$-torsors $Z\to X$, we may refine further.
\begin{definition}[descent obstruction]
	Fix a variety $X$ over $k$. For any smooth algebraic group $G$ over $k$, we set
	\[X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm H^1(k;G)}\coloneqq\bigcap_{f\in\mathrm H^1(k;G)}X(\AA)^f.\]
	We then define the \textit{descent obstruction} to be
	\[X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{descent}}=\bigcap_{\text{smooth affine }G}X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm H^1(k;G)}.\]
	We will say that $X$ has the \textit{descent obstruction} if and only if $X(\AA_k)\ne\emp$ while $X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{descent}}=\emp$.
\end{definition}
This sort of refinement holds in rather general situations: above, the only purpose of the group $G$ was to be able to write down $\mathrm H^1(-;G)$, which for our purposes could really be any functor $\mathrm{Sch}_k\opp\to\mathrm{Set}$.
\begin{notation}
	Suppose we have a functor $F\colon\mathrm{Sch}_k\opp\to\mathrm{Set}$, and then any $A\in F(X)$ comes with some evaluation maps $\op{ev}_A\colon X(k)\to F(k)$: any rational point $x\colon\Spec k\to X$ produces a map $F(X)\to F(k)$, which we denote by $A\mapsto\op{ev}_A(x)$.
\end{notation}

\[\begin{tikzcd}[cramped]

\begin{definition}
	Fix a variety $X$ over a number field $k$, and choose a functor $F\colon\mathrm{Sch}_k\opp\to\mathrm{Set}$. For any $A\in F(X)$, we define
	\[X(\AA_k)^A\coloneqq\{y\in X(\AA_k):\op{ev}_A(y)\subseteq\im(F(k)\to F(\AA_k)).\]
	We also define $X(\AA_k)^F\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{A\in F(X)}X(\AA_k)^A$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	Taking $F$ to be $\mathrm H^1(-;G)$ recovers the descent obstruction.
\end{example}
\begin{remark} \label{rem:functor-obstruction-natural}
	One can check this construction is functorial: a natural transformation $\eta\colon F\Rightarrow G$ produces a map $X(\AA_k)^F\to X(\AA_k)^G$. (In short, one sees that $X(\AA_k)^A\subseteq X(\AA_k)^{\eta(A)}$.) If the natural transformation is surjective, then the map is injective.
\end{remark}
Our application is the so-called Brauer--Manin obstruction.
\begin{definition}[Brauer--Manin obstruction]
	Let $X$ be a variety over a number field $k$. Then we define the \textit{Brauer--Manin obstruction} $X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{Br}}$ to be the obstruction produced by the functor
	\[\mathrm H^2_{\mathrm{\acute et}}(-;\mathbb G_m)\colon\mathrm{Sch}_k\opp\to\mathrm{Set}.\]
\end{definition}
It turns out that the Brauer--Manin obstruction can be seen as a version of the descent obstruction!
\begin{theorem}
	Let $X$ be a regular and quasiprojective variety over a number field $k$. Then
	\[X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{Br}}=\bigcap_{n\ge1}X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{PGL}_n}.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	The idea is that elements of the Brauer group are covered by $\mathrm H^1(X;\mathrm{PGL}_n)$s: the short exact sequence of sheaves
	\[1\to\mathbb G_m\to\mathrm{GL}_n\to\mathrm{PGL}_n\to1\]
	produces embeddings $\mathrm H^1(X;\mathrm{PGL}_n)\to\mathrm H^2(X;\mathbb G_m)$ for each $n$ (by Hilbert's theorem 90). Under the given assumptions, it turns out that elements of the Brauer group are all represented by Azumaya algebras, so this is surjective, provided that we sum over all $n$. The result now follows from \Cref{rem:functor-obstruction-natural}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Iskovskikh's Surface}
Let's work out an example of the descent obstruction. Consider the surface given by the equation
\[X\colon y^2+t^2=\left(x^2-2\right)\left(3-x^2\right),\]
which we embed into a weighted projective space as
\[X\colon Y^2+T^2=\left(X^2-2Z^2\right)\left(3Z^2-X^2\right),\]
where $\deg X=\deg Z=1$, and $\deg Y=\deg T=2$.
\begin{remark}
	One can show that $X$ admits points over $\QQ_p$ for all $p$. The moral is that $y^2+t^2$ attains every value in $\ZZ_p$ for all odd primes $p$, which one sees by computing which things are squares.
\end{remark}
To use the descent obstruction, we will need some torsors. Well, for $c\in\QQ^\times$, we define $Y_c$ by the equations
\[Y_c\colon\begin{cases}
	c\left(U^2+V^2\right)=X^2-2Z^2, \\
	c\left(W^2+S^2\right)=3Z^2-X^2.
\end{cases}\]
(Note that $Y_c$, up to isomorphism, only depends on $\QQ^\times/\im\op N_{\QQ(i)/\QQ}$.) We claim that the map $f_c\colon Y_c\to X$ defined by
\[f_c\colon(X,Z,Y,V,W,S)\mapsto(X,Z,c(UW-VS),c(US+VW))\]
is a torsor for the circle group $G\coloneqq\Spec\QQ[x,y]/\left(x^2+y^2-1\right)$. Indeed, the fiber over some $(X,Y,Z,T)$ will have $Y+iT=c(U+iV)(W+iS)$. Thus, we are hunting for two complex numbers $U+iV$ and $W+iS$, which the equations yield a norm requirement, and they have fixed product $Y+iT$. The locus of such complex numbers is exactly given by the circle group $G$!
\begin{example}
	At $c=1$, we see that $Z\coloneqq Y_1$ is cut out by the equations
	\[\begin{cases}
		U^2+V^2=X^2-2Z^2, \\
		W^2+S^2=3Z^2-X^2.
	\end{cases}\]
\end{example}
Now that we have a $G$-tosors, we should work to classify them.
\begin{lemma}
	Let $G$ be the circle group over $\QQ$. Then all $G$-torsors over $\QQ$ take the form
	\[T_c\coloneqq\Spec\QQ[u,v]/\left(u^2+v^2-c\right),\]
	where $c\in\QQ^\times$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	We need to compute $\mathrm H^1(k;G)$. Well, by definition, there is a short exact sequence
	\[1\to G\to\op{Res}_{\QQ(i)/\QQ}\mathbb G_m\stackrel{\op N}\to\mathbb G_m\to1,\]
	where the second map is given by the norm. The long exact sequence in cohomology yields
	\[\QQ(i)^\times\stackrel{\op N}\to\QQ^\times\to\mathrm H^1(\QQ;G)\to\mathrm H^1(\QQ;\op{Res}_{\QQ(i)/\QQ}\mathbb G_m).\]
	By unwinding Shapiro's lemma, we see that $\mathrm H^1(\QQ;\op{Res}_{\QQ(i)/\QQ}\mathbb G_m)=\mathrm H^1(\QQ(i);\mathbb G_m)$, which is $0$ by Hilbert's theorem 90. Thus, $\mathrm H^1(\QQ;G)$ is isomorphic to $\QQ^\times/\im\op N_{\QQ(i)/\QQ}$. A calculation of the boundary map and then realizing the $\mathrm H^1$ as torsors (which basically amounts to taking a pre-image), we find that the torsor corresponding to $c\in\QQ^\times/\im\op N_{\QQ(i)/\QQ}$ is given by $\Spec[u,v]/\left(u^2+v^2-c\right)$.
\end{proof}
To apply our descent obstruction, we now want to do a little twisting. Recall that we set $Z\coloneqq Y_1$, and one can calculate that the twist $Z^c$ is given by $Z\times^GT_{c^{-1}}$. There is an explicit isomorphism $Z^c\to Y_c$ given by
\[\left((X,Z,U+iV,W+iS),(A+iB)\right)\mapsto(X,Z,(A+iB)(U+iV),(A-iB)(W+iS)).\]
(One merely has to check that this is a $G$-equivariant map of torsors.) Thus, we see that our torsors $\{Y_c\}_c$ are all the twists of $Z$. The benefit of these equations is that one can show the following.
\begin{lemma}
	Fix everything as above. If $Y_c(\QQ)$ is nontrivial, then $c\equiv1\pmod{\QQ^{\times2}}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	After some care, we may assume that $X$ and $Z$ are coprime: for any prime factor $p$ which is $3\pmod4$, then $p^2\mid \left(U^2+V^2\right)$ forces $p$ to divide both the summands, so we may divide it out. But for any prime $p\equiv1\pmod4$, then we may divide $(U+iV)(U-iV)$ out by $(a+bi)(a-bi)$ where $p=a^2+b^2$. We then do some case-by-case analysis. One can rule out solutions at $X=0$ or $Z=0$ by looking over $\RR$. Once $X$ and $Z$ are coprime, we know $c=1$.
\end{proof}
It remains to study $Y_1(\QQ)$. Rearranging the second equation, we see that
\[3=\left(\frac XZ\right)^2+\left(\frac WZ\right)^2+\left(\frac SZ\right)^2.\]
If $v_2(X/Z)>0$, then $3-\left(\frac XZ\right)^2\equiv-1\pmod4$, which creates a contradiction. If $v_2(X/Z)=0$, then we see that $\left(\frac XZ\right)^2-2\equiv-1\pmod4$, and we use the first equation. Lastly, if $v_2(X/Z)<0$, then $3(Z/X)^2-1\equiv-1\pmod4$, and we again have a contradiction.

We conclude that $X(\QQ)=\emp$. Indeed, we have seen that $Z^c(\QQ)=\emp$ for all $c$, so $X(\AA_\QQ)^G$ is empty.
\begin{remark}
	There is a faster proof because $Y^2+T^2$ needs to be a sum of two squares, so it must have an even number of prime factors which are $3\pmod4$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Affine Deligne--Lusztig Induction}
Here are our two stories.
\begin{itemize}
	\item In the finite-dimensional setting, we fix a Borel subgroup $B\subseteq G$ over $\FF_q$ and some parabolic $B$ of $G$. Then there is a Newton map $G/\op{Ad}_\sigma B\to G/\op{Ad}_\sigma G$, and its fibers look like $G/B$. The target is $\mathrm BG(\FF_q)$.

	\[R^\theta(w)\coloneqq\sum_i(-1)^i\mathrm H^i_c(X(w);\mc F_\theta),\]
	where $\theta$ is a character of the torus, and $\mc F_\theta$ is some specified local system on $X_w(1)$. In terms of the derived category, we may write $R_w^*=\mathrm H^\bullet(X_w(1);\overline\QQ_\ell)$ and $R_w^!=\mathrm H^\bullet_c(X_w(1);\overline\QQ_\ell)$. Roughly speaking, these are pushforwards of the constant sheaves along the Newton map from a stratum.

	One also has a covering $\widetilde{X_w(1)}\to X_w(1)$ which is a $T^{wF}$-torsor, defined as follows: note that we can think of $X_w(1)$ as $\left\{gB\in G/B:g^{-1}\sigma(g)\in BwB\right\}$, so one can define the cover as
	\[\widetilde{X_w(1)}=\left\{gU\in G/U:g^{-1}\sigma(g)\in UwU\right\}.\]

	\item In the affine setting, we use the Iwahori subgroup $I$ of the loop group $LG$, and we have a parabolic $L^+P\subseteq LG$. In this case, our Newton map looks like $\mathrm{Sht}_P\to\mathrm{Isoc}_G$, which has fibers which look like $LG/L^+P$.

	Here, the Newton stratification $X_{\le w}(b)$ of local shtukas gives the affine Deligne--Lusztig varieties. Then we have affine Deligne--Lusztig induction given by $R_w^*=\op{Nt}_*(i_{w*}\omega_{\mathrm{Sht}_w}[-\ell(\omega)])$. There is also a covering $\widetilde{\mathrm{Sht}_w}\to\mathrm{Sht}_w$.

\subsection{Categorical Trace}
Let's start by talking about monodromy sheaves. Here is the finite version.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a connected reductive group $H$ over $\overline\FF_q$. Then we define the \textit{Serre's fundamental group} as $\pi_1^c(H)$ to be the profinite group
	\[\lim_{H'\to H}\ker(H'\to H),\]
	where $H'\to H$ is a finite \'etale connected cover.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	By construction, $\pi_1^c(H)$ is covered by $\pi_1^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(H)$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	If $H$ is defined over $\FF_q$, then $\op{Gal}(\overline\FF_q/\FF_q)$ naturally acts on these kernels, so it naturally acts on $\pi_1^c(H)$.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	If $H=\op{PGL}_p$, then $\pi_1^c(H)=1$. For example, the covering $\op{SL}_p\to\op{PGL}_p$ is not \'etale!
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	If $H$ is commutative (such as an abelian variety), then one can take rather general quotients, so one finds that $\pi_1^c(H)=\lim_nH(\FF_{q^n})$.
\end{example}
This generality allows us to define a character sheaf.
\begin{definition}[character sheaf]
	Fix a reductive group $H$ over $\overline\FF_q$. A \textit{character sheaf} is a rank $1$ local system $\mc F$ on $H$, along with the data of a multiplication
	\[m^*\mc F\cong\mc F\boxtimes\mc F,\]
	and some cocycle condition.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $H$ is connected, then it turns out that the groupoid of character sheaves is discrete. We can represent them as follows; for any continuous homomorphism $\chi\colon\pi_1^c(H)\to\Lambda^\times$, we can produce a rank $1$ local system via the covering $\pi_1^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(H)\to\pi_1^c(H)$. This turns out to give a bijection. One can categorify this construction to a functor
	\[\op{Ch}\colon\op{Coh}(R_{\pi_1^c(H),\mathbb G_m})^\heartsuit\to\op{Shv}(H;\Lambda)^\heartsuit.\]
\end{example}
So here is our definition of a monodromy sheaf.
\begin{definition}
	A \textit{monodromy sheaf} is the full subcategory generated by the heart of the previous example. If $X$ is a prestack with action of $H$, then the monodromy sheaves on $X$ are the functors $\op{Shv}_{\mathrm{mon}}(H)\to\mathrm{Shv}(X)$.
\end{definition}
Return to finite groups, it turns out that Deligne--Lusztig induction can be viewed as providing a functor $\op{Ch}_{G,\varphi}^{\mathrm{mon}}$ from $\mathrm{Shv}(U\backslash G/U)\to\op{Rep}G(\FF_q)$ by the correspondence
\[U\backslash G/U\from G/\op{Ad}_\sigma G\stackrel{\op{Nt}}\to G/\op{Ad}_\sigma G.\]
A similar correspondnce produces a functor $\op{Ch}_{G,\varphi}^{\mathrm{unip}}\colon\op{IndShv}_c(B\backslash G/B)\to\op{Rep}G(\FF_q)$. It will turn out that taking the ``categorical trace'' makes these functors equivalences. (The second functor actually gives an equivalence to $\op{IndRep}_c^{\mathrm{unip}}G(\FF_q)$.)
\begin{definition}
	Fix an associative algebra object $A$ in the category of correspondences of some geometric objects, and let $F$ be an $A$-bimodule. Then we define
	\[\op{Tr}(D(A),D(F))\coloneqq D(A)\otimes_{D(A)\otimes_{D(\mathrm{pt})}D(A)^{\mathrm{rev}}}D(F),\]
	where $D$ is some sheaf theory. There is also a \textit{geometric trace}
	\[\op{Tr}_{\mathrm{geo}}(D(A),D(F))\coloneqq D(\left|\op{Bar}(A)_\bullet\otimes_{A\otimes A^{\mathrm{rev}}}F\right|).\]
	We may write this as $D(\op{Tr}(A,F))$
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	In good situations, the trace and geometric trace agree.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	A choice of algebra endomorphism $\varphi\colon A\to A$ makes $A$ into an $A$-bimodule, where $A$ acts on the left by $\varphi$ and on the right by $A^{\mathrm{rev}}$.
\end{remark}
We will discuss the affine situation next time, which is the following result.
\begin{theorem}
	There is an equialence
	\[\op{Tr}(\op{Shv}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathrm{Iw}^u\backslash LG/\mathrm{Iw}^u),\varphi)\cong\op{Shv}^{\mathrm{tame}}(\mathrm{Isoc}_G),\]
	where tame means that it comes from a depth-zero representation.
\end{theorem}

\section{March 17: Moments of Quadratic \texorpdfstring{$L$}{ L}-functions}
This talk was given by Adrian Diaconu at Boston College for the BC--MIT number theory seminar.

\subsection{The Analytic Conjectures}
We are working over function fiels today, and we are interested in Dirichlet $L$-functions (i.e., $L$-functions attached to automorphic forms of $\op{GL}_1$). Here is the conjecture over $\QQ$.
\begin{conj}
	Let $\chi_d$ be the Kronecker symbol $\left(\frac\cdot d\right)$. THen
	\[{\sum_{0<d<D}}^*L(1/2,\chi_d)^r=\frac3\pi DQ_r(\log D)+o(D).\]
\end{conj}
This is known for $r\le3$ classically; there is also a second-order term known for $r=3$, which is similar to the second-order term found when counting cubic number fields. There are some partial results for $r=4$.

We now turn to function fields.
\begin{definition}
	Fix $d,m\in\FF_q[x]$ with $m$ monic, and then we define $\chi_d$ to be the $d$th Kronecker symbol. Then we define
	\[L(s,\chi_d)=\sum_{f}\frac{\chi_d(f)}{\left|f\right|^s},\]
	where $\left|f\right|=q^{\deg f}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{conj}
	As $g\to\infty$, we have
	\[\frac1{q^{2g+1}}\sum_{\substack{\text{monic, squarefree }d\\\deg d=2g+1}}L(1/2,\chi_d)^r=Q_r(2g+1)+o(1).\]
\end{conj}
We again know about the same: this is known for $r\le3$, and there are some asymptotics known for $r=4$. The reason that we do not see much improvement is because there is not an easy way to relate this problem to geometry.

Let's say something about the polynomial $Q_r$, which has a conjectural decomposition.
\begin{conj}
	Fix $r\ge3$, and choose integers $D,N\ge1$ for which $(N+1)^{-1}<\Theta<N^{-1}$. Then
	\[\sum_{\substack{\text{monic, squarefree }d\\\deg d=D}}L(1/2,\chi_d)^r=\sum_{n\le N}q^{(\frac12+\frac1{2n})D}Q_{n,r}(D,q)+O_{\Theta,q,r}\left(q^{D(1+\Theta)/2}\right).\]
\end{conj}
This is known for $r=3$ (even for number fields), and there are partial results for $r=4$.

Our main result is a proof of the asymptotic formula in the ``large $q$'' regime.
\begin{theorem}
	For an odd prime-power $q$, one has
	\[\frac1{q^{2g+1}}\sum_{\substack{\text{monic, squarefree }d\\\deg d=D}}L(1/2,\chi_d)^r=Q_r(2g+1)+O\left(4^{g(r+1)}q^{(17-g)/34}\right).\]
\end{theorem}
Morally, this is a refinement of the following result.
\begin{theorem}[Katz--Sarnak]
	Fix $g$. Then
	\[\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac1{q^{2g}(q-1)}\sum_{\substack{\text{monic, squarefree }d\\\deg d=D}}L(1/2,\chi_d)^r=\int_{\op{USp}(2g)}\det(1-A)^r\,dA.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	The method has been extended to handle ratios of products of $L$-functions by Victor Wang.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Configuration Spaces}
We are interested in the following spaces.
\begin{definition}
	Let $\op{Conf}_n(\CC)$ be the configuration space of $n$ distinct ordered points, which is equivalent to the space of degree-$n$ monic polynomials with no repeated roots.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that $\pi_1\op{Conf}_n(\CC)$ is Artin's braid group on $n$ strands, denoted $\beta_n$. For $n\ge2$, it turns out that $\op{Conf}_n(\CC)$ is a $K(\beta_n,1)$.
\end{remark}
\begin{notation}
	There is a canonical local system $d\mapsto\mathrm H^1(\op{Conf}_n(\CC);\CC)$, which we denote by $V$.
\end{notation}
It will turn out that we want to understand $\mathrm H^*(\op{Conf}_{2g+1}(\CC);V)=\mathrm H^*(\beta_{2g+1};V)$. We can also replace $V$ with other representations $V_\lambda$ of $\op{Sp}_{2g}$. This must be a hard problem because it solves our question, as we will explain.
\begin{remark}
	One can identify $\op{Conf}_{2g+1}\left(\AA^1\right)/\mathbb G_a$ with the moduli space $\mc H_{2g+1}$ of hyperelliptic curves with $2g+1$ branch points, along with a marked Weierstrass point and a nonzero tangent vector there.
\end{remark}
The moral is that
\[\tr\left(\mathrm{Frob}_q;\mathrm H_\bullet^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(\beta_{2g+1};V_\lambda(\left|\lambda\right|/2))\right)=\frac1{q^{2g+1}}\sum_{\substack{\text{monic, squarefree }d\\\deg d=D}}s_{\langle\lambda\rangle}\left(q^{-1/2}\Theta_d\right),\]
where $s_{\langle\lambda\rangle}$ is a symplectic Schur function, and $\Theta_d$ is the multiset of our Frobenius eigenvalues for the hyperelliptic curve $y^2=d(x)$. On the other hand, one can show that
\[\frac1{q^{2g+1}}\sum_{\substack{\text{monic, squarefree }d\\\deg d=D}}L(t_1;\chi_d)\cdots L(t_r;\chi_d)=(t_1\cdots t_r)^g\sum_{\lambda}\tr_\lambda(g)s_{\lambda^\dagger}(q^{\pm1/2}t_1^{\pm1},\ldots,q^{\pm1/2}t_r^{\pm1}),\]
where $\tr_\lambda(g)$ is the Frobenius trace from the previous equation. Thus, our goal has become to compute some homology groups with uniformity in $\lambda$, so the problem is now one in homotopy theory.
\begin{remark}
	We remark that the ``source'' of our homological stability is from Borel's result in automorphic forms that there is homological stability for algebraic representations of arithmetic groups. One uses homotopy theory to pull back the stability from the arithmetic groups to the braid groups.
\end{remark}

\section{March 17: Elliptic Curves of Small Rank}
This talk was given by David Zywina at Boston College for the BC--MIT seminar.

\subsection{The Lower Bound}
Fix a number field $K$, like $\QQ$, and we choose an elliptic curve $E$ over $K$. It is known that $E(K)$ is a finitely generated abelian group, and we are interested in understanding its rank. For example, it is not known which ranks occur (e.g., are they bounded?) or how often (e.g., which ranks can occur infinitely often?).
\begin{example}
	For $K=\QQ$, the highest known rank is $29$. The consensus seems to change on whether ranks should be bounded.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	There is a heuristic that says tthat ranks up to $21$ will occur infinitely often (from elliptic curves over $\QQ$), and there are only finitely many elliptic curves with ranks more than $21$.
\end{example}
Here is our main result.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a number field $K$ and integer $r\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$. Then there are infinitely many elliptic curves (up to geometric isomorphism) over $K$ with rank $r$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Mazur and Rubin showed the case $r=0$. The case of $r=1$ was independently proven by Koymans--Pagano. There were also special cases known for some fields.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	One can find infinitely many elliptic curves of lower-bounded rank. For example, Elkies found infinitely many elliptic curves of rank at least $19$. Thus, the main content of our result is to be able to upper-bound ranks.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture is only known for finitely many elliptic curves of rank greater than $1$. Here, there is a difficulty that we cannot compute these analytic ranks without careful calculation.
\end{remark}
We will focus on the $r=4$ case today. Basically, the only way to compute a rank is to lower-bound by finding explicit points, upper-bound by computing some Selmer groups, and then hope for an equality. Thus, let's start by choosing a special elliptic surface, which we engineer to have many points.
\begin{example}
	We define the elliptic surface $\mc E$ over $K(T)$ by $y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$, where
	\[\begin{cases}
		a=-70\left(T^2-25\right), \\
		b=2^4\cdot7^2\left(T^2-11^2\right)\left(T^2-25^2\right).
	\end{cases}\]
	For example, it turns out that the discriminant splits completely as
	\[2^4\cdot3^2\cdot7^6(T-11)^2(T+11)^2(T-25)^3(T+25)^3(T-39)(T+39).\]
	(This is something very remarkable!) One can calculate that $\mc E(K(T))\cong\ZZ/2\ZZ\times\ZZ^4$, and one can explicitly compute what generates the $\ZZ^4$.
\end{example}
The idea is to plug in various values of $T$ to produce an elliptic curve $\mc E_t$ over $K$; note that we should avoid $t\in\{\pm11,\pm25,\pm39\}$ to continue to an elliptic curve. We are able to lower-bound the rank thanks to the following.
\begin{theorem}[Silverman]
	Fix an elliptic surface $\mc E$ over $K(T)$. For all but finitely many $t\in K$, one has
	\[\op{rank}(\mc E/K(T))\le\op{rank}(\mc E_t/K).\]
\end{theorem}
The moral is that one can ``plug in'' the generating points from $\mc E$ and (generically) receive linearly independent points in $\mc E_t$.

\subsection{The Upper Bound}
It remains to upper-bound the ranks of the $\mc E_t$ for infinitely many $t$, for which we will use Selmer groups. (One also knows that eacch $j$-invariant only appears in $\mc E_t$ finitely many times, so we receive different isomorphism classes of elliptic curves.)

\begin{theorem}[Kai]
	Fix everything as in the previous paragraph. There are nonzero $m,n\in\OO_{K,S}$ satisfying the following.
	\begin{listroman}
		\item For each $e\in\{\pm11,\pm25,\pm39\}$, the elements $m+en$ generate distinct prime ideals of $\OO_{K,S}$.
		\item We have $(m,n)\in U_{\mf p}$ for each $\mf p\in S$.
		\item We have $m/n\in U_v$ for each $v\mid\infty$.
	\end{listroman}
\end{theorem}
This talk has no interest in the proof of this theorem, but we will use to select our $t$s. For example, let's assume that $S$ contains enough primes to make $\OO_{K,S}$ a principal ideal domain, and we assume that it contains all primes above $2$, $3$, and $7$. By adjusting the local conditions $U_{\mf p}$, we can more or less control if the primes in $S$ are actually bad reduction.

We have the following results about generalized Fermat equations.
\begin{theorem} \label{thm:generalized-fermat}
	Fix positive integers $p$, $q$, and $r$, and choose nonzero integers $A$, $B$, and $C$. Set $\chi\coloneqq\frac1p+\frac1q+\frac1r-1$, and we will consider the curve $X\colon Ax^p+By^q=Cz^r$.
	\begin{listalph}
		\item Beukers: if $\chi>0$, then this equation has either zero or infinitely many primitive solutions in a finite collection of two-parameter families.
		\item If $\chi=0$, then this equation reduces to torsors of elliptic curves.
		\item Darmon--Granville: if $\chi>0$, then there are finitely many primitive solutions.
	\end{listalph}
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	Part (b) has little content: there are finitely many $(p,q,r)$ with $\chi=0$, and one can check that they yield elliptic curves.
\end{remark}
We are only going to focus on showing (c) of \Cref{thm:generalized-fermat}. It will follow from the following analogous trichotomy.
\begin{theorem}
	Let $C$ be a projective curve over $\QQ$ of genus $g$, and set $\chi\coloneqq2-2g$.
	\begin{listalph}
		\item Hasse--Minkowski: if $\chi>0$, then $C(\QQ)$ is either empty or infinite and can be explained by the local-to-global principle.
		\item Mordell--Weil: if $\chi=0$, then $C(\QQ)$ is either empty or a finitely generated abelian group.
		\item Faltings: if $\chi>0$, then $C(\QQ)$ is finite.
	\end{listalph}
\end{theorem}
It turns out that Faltings's theorem implies \Cref{thm:generalized-fermat}, but one has to be careful because $Ax^p+By^q=Cz^r$ is not a projective cure in general.

For convenience, we will assume that $p$, $q$, and $r$ are pairwise coprime, and for simplicity, we will assume that $A=B=C=1$. Then one can certainly use the equation $x^p+y^q=z^r$ to define some surface in $\AA^3_\QQ\setminus\{0\}$. In the case where $p=q=r$, we can take a quotient by the $\mathbb G_m$-action given by $(x,y,z)\mapsto(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda z)$, so we receive a closed subscheme of $\PP^2$, which is our projective curve. In general, we are only stable under the $\mathbb G_m$-action given by
\[\lambda\colon(x,y,z)\mapsto(\lambda^{qr}x,\lambda^{pr}y,\lambda^{pq}z)\]
Accordingly, we may pass to the quotient stack $[S/\mathbb G_m]$ in weighted projective space. Up to rearranging $(p,q,r)$, we find that all stabilizers of points in $[S/\mathbb G_m]$ are trivial, except for a stabilizer $\mu_q$ at $(1,0,1)$, a stabilizer $\mu_r$ at $(1,-1,0)$, and a stabilizer $\mu_p$ at $(0,1,1)$.

We will now hunt for a finite \'etale cover $X$ of $[S/\mathbb G_m]$, where $X$ is actually a scheme. We will then apply Faltings's theorem to $X$ to produce our finiteness. The existence of this $X$ will be conjured using complex analysis: finding such a cover $X\to[S/\mathbb G_m]$ is equivalent to finding a cover $X\to\PP^1$ (of complex algebraic varieties) which is ramified above $0$, $1$, and $\infty$, with degrees $p$, $q$, and $r$, respectively. We will use the following tool.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a smooth complex variety $Y$. Then the category of finite \'etale covers of $Y$ is equivalent to the category of finite covering spaces of $Y(\CC)$.
\end{theorem}
We will apply this theorem to $Y\coloneqq[S/\mathbb G_m]\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}$, which is a genuine complex affine curve. Then $\pi_1(Y)=\langle\gamma_0,\gamma_1,\gamma_\infty:\gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_\infty=1\rangle$. It turns out that $\pi_1(Y)$ admits a finite-index subgroup for which $\gamma_0$, $\gamma_1$, and $\gamma_\infty$ have orders $p$, $q$, and $r$ in the quotient. Thus, we receive a finite \'etale cover $X^\circ\to Y$ with the corresponding fundamental group. Then there is a smooth projective curve $X$ containing $X^\circ$ so that $X^\circ\to Y$ extends uniquely to $X\to\PP^1$ with the desired ramifications. This then passes to some \'etale cover $X\to[S/\mathbb G_m]$. We remark that (a priori) $X$ is only defined over $\CC$, but it surely can descend to $\overline\QQ$ and then to a number field $K$.

We now apply Faltings's theorem to $X$, for which we will use the following tool.
\begin{theorem}[Beckmann]
	Let $\pi\colon X\to[S/\mathbb G_m]$ be the above covering. For each $t\in S(\ZZ)$, the fiber over $t$ in $X$ is defined over an extension $L$ of $K$ of degree at most $\deg\pi$, where $L$ is unramified away from $pqr$.
\end{theorem}
Thus, we see that $\#S(\ZZ)\le\#X(L)$, where $L$ is the composite of all extensions $L$ of $K$ with degree bounded by $\deg\pi$ and unramified away from $pqr$. There are only finitely many such extensions, so $L$ is finite over $\QQ$, so $X(L)$ is finite by Faltings's theorem, so we are done! (Note $\chi(X)<0$ by a calculation with Riemann--Hurwitz.)

The Euler characteristic of $X$ is negative, so we see that we need to get the upper-half plane $\mc H$. Thus, we see that we are hunting for a quotient of $\mc H$.
\begin{example}
	The natural action of $\op{PSL}_2(\RR)$ on $\mc H$ (by fractional linear transformations) induces an analytic map $\op{PSL}_2(\ZZ)\backslash\mc H\to\PP^1(\CC)$.
\end{example}
The map $\op{PSL}_2(\ZZ)\backslash\mc H\to\PP^1(\CC)$ fails to be surjective. To fix this, we add in cusps.
\begin{notation}

\end{notation}

\begin{itemize}

	\item The point $i$ has stabilizer $\mu_2$.
	\item The point $\omega$ has stabilizer $\mu_3$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}

In order to descend to a curve over $\QQ$, we define $X(7)$ as a curve over $\QQ$ which classifies elliptic curves $E$, together with an isomorphism $\varphi\colon\mu_7\times\ZZ/7\ZZ\to E[7]$ which is an isomorphism of symplectic spaces.
\end{remark}
We would now like to use this cover to compute points on $S\colon x^2+y^3=z^7$. The approach of the previous subsection would have us compute points on $X(7)$ in some field extensions of degree equal to the degree of the map $X(7)\to\PP^1$, which is the huge value $\frac12[\Gamma(1):\Gamma(7)]=168$. Thus, we are motivated to find such twists. Twists come from $\mathrm H^1(\QQ;\op{Aut}(X(7)/X(1)))$. This automorphism group is basically given by symplectic automorphisms of $\mu_7\times\ZZ/7\ZZ$ (up to $\{\pm1\}$). For example, any choice of elliptic curve $E_0$ can be equipped with an isomorphism $E_0[7]\cong\ZZ/7\ZZ\times\mu_7$, at least over the algebraic closure. This data is enough to define a twist of $X(7)$, which is the moduli space of elliptic curves $E$ equipped with a symplectic isomorphism $E[7]\cong E_0[7]$.

More or less by construction, we receive a short exact sequence
\[1\to X_*(T)_{I_F}\to\widetilde W\to W_0\to1.\]
Now, let $W_{\mathrm{aff}}$ be the subgroup generated by the affine reflections; it turns out that $\widetilde W/W_{\mathrm{aff}}$ is isomorphic to $\pi_1(G)_{I_F}$. Accordingly, there is a map $\widetilde W\to\pi_1(G)_{I_F}$ given by the projection. There is also a map $\widetilde W\to X_*(T)_\QQ^{I_F}$ given by sending $w$ to
\[\frac1n\left(w\cdot\sigma w\cdots\sigma^{n-1}w\right),\]
where $n$ is chosen large enough so that this element is in $X_*(T)^{I_F}_\QQ$.

Now, choose a parabolic $\check P$ containing $T_{\check F}$, whose Levi decomposition we denote by $\check M\check U$.

There is a length function $\ell$ on $\check W$ induced by its decomposition as a semidirect product $X_*(T)_{I_F}\rtimes W_0$.
\begin{definition}
	We say that $\sigma\in\widetilde W$ is \textit{$\sigma$-straight} if and only if $\ell(w)=\langle2\rho,\nu_w\rangle$, where $\nu_w$ is the image of $w$ in $X_*(T)_\QQ^{I_F}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It is equivalent to ask for
	\[\ell\left(w\cdot\sigma w\cdots\sigma^{n-1}w\right)=n\ell(w)\]
	for all $n>0$. Thus, we are morally asking for $w$ and its conjugates to have no cancellation when we multiply them together.
\end{remark}
We also need a little combinatorics.
\begin{definition}
	We write $w\stackrel t\to_\sigma w'$ if and only if $w'=tw\sigma(t)^{-1}$ and $\ell(t)\le1$ and $\ell(w')\le\ell(w)$. Furthemore, we may just write $w\to_\sigma w'$ if there is a suitable path between the two Weyl elements. If $w\to_\sigma w'$ and $w'\to_\sigma w$, we say that $w$ and $w'$ are \textit{conjugate by cyclic shift}.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[He--Nie] \label{thm:he-nie}
	Fix a conjugacy class $C$ in $\widetilde W$.
	\begin{listalph}
		\item If $C$ is $\sigma$-straight, then $C_{\min}$ is the set of $\sigma$-straight elements.
		\item For all $\nu\in C$, there is a path $\nu\to\nu_n$ given by simple reflections, and there is a decomposition with $\nu_n=uw$ where $w$ is $\sigma$-straight, and $u$ is in the Weyl group $W_{\check f}$ of some alcove $\check f$, and $w\cdot\sigma W_{\check f}\cdot w^{-1}=W_{\check f}$.
	\end{listalph}
\end{theorem}
We are interested in classifying the $\sigma$-conjugacy classes in $\widetilde W$. Let $B(\widetilde W)$ denote this set.
\begin{theorem}[He] \label{thm:he}
	The set $B(\widetilde W)$ is in bijection with the collection $Q$ of quadruples $(M,x,\check f_M,\mf c)$, where
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $M$ is a Levi subgroup of a standard parabolic $P$ for which $T\subseteq M$,
		\item $x\in\widetilde W_M$ has $\ell_M(x)=0$, and $\widetilde\nu-x$ is dominant, and $Z_G(\nu_x)=M$,
		\item $\check f_M$ is  a facet of the alcove which is $\op{Ad}_x\sigma$-stable, and
		\item $\mf c\in W_{\check f_M}$ is an elliptic $\op{Ad}_x\sigma$-conjugacy class.
	\end{itemize}
	The bijection sends a quadruple $(M,x,\check f_M,\mf c)$ to the class of $\mf c\cdot x$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{example}
	Consider $G=\op{SL}_2(F)$, where $\widetilde W$ is an infinite dihedral group, generated by two simple reflections $s_0$ and $s_1$. This group is split, so $B(\widetilde W)$ is simply the conjugacy classes.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item There is the conjugacy class of $1$, which is the quadruple $(G,1,e,1)$, where $e$ is an edge of the building.
		\item There is the conjugacy class of $s_0$, which is $(G,1,v_0,s_0)$. (Note $W_{v_0}=\langle s_0\rangle$.)
		\item The quadruple for $s_1$ is similar.
		\item There is lastly the conjugacy class of translations $t_n$ (by $2n$, for $n\ge1$). Here, our Levi must be $T$, so $\widetilde W_T$ is just $\ZZ$. We find that we need to take $x=t_n$, and the rest of the quadruple has no content.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\subsection{Drinfeld Modules}
The Tamagawa number formula is intended to generalize the Dirichlet class number formula. The equivariant Tamagawa number formula adds in some Galois action.
\begin{conj}[Equivariant Tamagawa number]
	FIx an abelian extension $K/F$ of number fields with Galois group $G$. Define an $L$-function $\Theta_{K/F}\colon\CC\to\CC[G]$ to be given by
	\[\Theta_{K/F}(s)=\sum_\chi L(\chi,s)e_{\chi^{-1}}.\]
	Then
	\[\Theta_{K/F}^*(0)\ZZ[G]=\op{triv}_{\RR[G]}\op{det}_{\ZZ[G]}C_F^\bullet,\]
	where $C_F^\bullet$ is some perfect complex of $\ZZ[G]$-modules.
\end{conj}
There is a Tamagawa number formula for Drinfeld $t$-modules, and there is even an equivariant version. This equivariant version is our target.

Let $q$ be a power of $p$, set $A\coloneqq\FF_q[t]$ and $k\coloneqq\FF_q(t)$. Let $K/F$ be an abelian extension of function fields over $k$, with Galois group $G$, and we will assume that their field of constants is $\FF_q$.
\begin{definition}[Drinfeld]
	A \textit{Drinfeld module} $E$ over $\OO_F$ is a morphism $\varphi_E\colon A\to\OO_F\{\tau\}$ (where $\tau(x)=x^q$) of $\FF_q$-algebras such that $\varphi_E(t)$ has constant term $t$. The degree of $\varphi_E$ is the \textit{rank} of $E$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Extension of scalars upgrades a Drinfeld module to a functor.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	For each place $v$, there is a Tate module
	\[T_{v_0}E\coloneqq\lim_nE(\overline F)[v_0^n],\]
	which has a natural Galois action.
\end{remark}
As with elliptic curves, these Tate modules assemble into a family of representations
\[\mathrm H^1(E;A_{v_0}[G])\coloneqq T_{v_0}E^*\otimes_{A_{v_0}}A_{v_0}[G],\]
which has the natural diagonal Galois action.
\begin{remark}
	There are compatibility conditions on these Galois representations.
\end{remark}
We now work to define an equivariant $L$-function. Our analogue of $\RR$ is $k_\infty\coloneqq\FF_q((1/t))$, and our analogue of $\CC$ is $\widehat{\overline{k_\infty}}$. Note that $k_\infty$ has a subgroup of monic elements, which is our analogue of $\RR^+$.
\begin{notation}
	Set $\mathbb S_\infty\coloneqq\CC_\infty^\times\times\ZZ_p$. Then we define $\Theta^E_{K/F}$ to be
	\[\prod_v^*P_v^G\left(\op Nv^{-s}\right),\]
	where $P_v^G$ is a characteristic polynomial. (Here, the $*$ means that we are only taking a product over the tamely ramified places which have good reduction for $E$.)
\end{notation}
Here is our first special value result.
\begin{proposition}
	In $k_\infty[G]^+$, one has
	\[\Theta_{K/F}^E(0)=\prod_v\frac{\left|\OO_K/v\right|_G}{\left|E(\OO_K/v)\right|_G}.\]
\end{proposition}
To continue, we need to complete our Euler product. The idea is to plug in $\mc M/v$ into $E$ for some projective module $\mc M$, instead of $\OO_K/v$; then we go ahead and defien $\Theta_{K/F}^{E,\mc M}$ to be the full product. After defining an Arakelov class, one is able to state and prove an equivariant Tamagawa number formula.

	\[\lim_n\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}(\op{SL}_n(\RR);\RR)\to\lim_n\mathrm H^*(\op{SL}_n(\ZZ);\RR)\]
	given by restriction of cocycles is an isomorphism.
\end{theorem}
The target can be computed by analytic techniques (with Lie algebra cohomology) as $\land^*(b_5,b_9,\ldots)$ where $\deg b_i=i$, so we find that
\[K_i(\ZZ)_\QQ=\begin{cases}
	\QQ & \text{if }i\equiv1\pmod4, \\
	0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}\]
Namely, $K$-theory is just primitive elements in the group cohomology.

We are interested in finding a $p$-adic analogue.
\begin{conj} \label{conj:compute-k-group-p-adic}
	The natural map
	\[\lim_n\mathrm H^*(\op{SL}_n(\ZZ_p);\QQ_p)\to\lim_n\mathrm H^*(\op{SL}_n(\ZZ);\QQ_p)\]
	is surjective.
\end{conj}
Again, one can compute the left-hand side as $\land^*(\ell_3,\ell_5,\ldots)$ where $\deg b_i=i$, again using some Lie algebra cohomology techiques. This was done by Lazard.
\begin{remark}
	These natural maps are maps of Hopf algebras, so it turns out to be enough to merely send primitive elements to primitive elements. Namely, the archimedean calculation is enough to show that the natural restriction map on primitive elements from $K_{4k+1}(\ZZ)_{\QQ_p}$ to $K_{4k+1}^{\mathrm{cont}}(\ZZ_p)_\QQ$ is an isomorphism. This is interesting to number theorists because it is equivalent to the natural map
	\[\mathrm H^1(\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ/\QQ);\QQ_p(2k+1))\to\mathrm H^1(\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ_p/\QQ_p);\QQ_p(2k+1)).\]
\end{remark}
We have nothing new to say about this conjecture.
\begin{remark}
	For $n\ge3$, every finite-index subgroup of $\op{SL}_n(\ZZ)$ contains a congruence subgroup. Thus, the profinite completion of $\op{SL}_n(\ZZ)$ is the limit of the groups $\op{SL}_n(\ZZ/N\ZZ)$ (using some strong approximation), which is $\op{SL}_n(\widehat\ZZ)\cong\prod_p\op{SL}_n(\ZZ_p)$. For continuity reasons, it follows that
	\[\mathrm H^*(\op{SL}_n(\ZZ);\QQ_p)\cong\mathrm H^*(\widehat{\op{SL}_n(\ZZ)};\QQ_p)\]
\end{remark}
Thus, we see that we can take a profinite completion on the right-hand side of \Cref{conj:compute-k-group-p-adic}. It will turn out that there are some interesting homotopy groups at play. To see this, we recall the definition of the profinite completion of a space.
\begin{definition}[profinite completion]
	Fix a space $X$. Then the \textit{profinite completion} of $X$ is the limit over all functors $X\to F$, where $F$ is a finite space.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	One has $\pi_1(\widehat X;x)=\widehat{\pi_1(X;x)}$, but this might not be true for higher homotopy groups.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	Profinite completion does not change cohomology with finite coefficients. If one defines
	\[\mathrm R\Gamma(\widehat X;\ZZ_p)\coloneqq\mathrm R\lim\mathrm R\Gamma(\widehat X;\ZZ/p^n\ZZ),\]
	then this can again be read off of the cohomology of $X$.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	Set $X\coloneqq\mathrm B\Gamma$, where $\Gamma$ is some discrete group. Then
	\[\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}(\widehat\Gamma;\QQ_p)=\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}\left(\pi_1(\widehat{\mathrm B\Gamma});\QQ_p\right).\]
	This then maps to $\mathrm H^*(\widehat{\mathrm B\Gamma};\QQ_p)$, which is the interesting map. If $\Gamma$ has $\mathrm H^i(\Gamma;\ZZ/m\ZZ)$ finite for all $i$ and $m$ (for example, if $\Gamma$ is finite already), then we can descend to $\mathrm H^*(\mathrm B\Gamma;\QQ_p)$, which is $\mathrm H^*(\Gamma;\QQ_p)$. Thus, the general question of whether this long composite is an isomorphism should be measured by $\widehat{\mathrm B\Gamma}$; for example, if it is not an isomorphism, then $\pi_i(\widehat{\mathrm B\Gamma})\ne0$ for some $i$.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	Conversely, one can use \Cref{conj:compute-k-group-p-adic} to compute homotopy groups of $\widehat{\mathrm B\op{SL}_n(\ZZ)}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The \'Etale Homotopy Type}
Here is our definition.
\begin{definition}[\'etale homotopy type]
	There is a functor $(-)_{\mathrm{\acute et}}$ which takes schemes and outputs profinite spaces.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that $Y_{\mathrm{\acute et}}$ remembers cohomology, and it remembers the (\'etale) fundamental group.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	If $Y$ is finite type over a subfield $F$ of $\CC$, then the \'etale homotopy type is immune to base-change, so we may assume $F=\CC$. Then we define $X_{\CC,\mathrm{\acute et}}$ as the profinite completion of $X(\CC)$. For example, $X_{\mathrm{\acute et}}$ automatically has an action by $\op{Gal}(\overline F/F)$, which recovers the Galois actoin on the \'etale cohomology groups.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	For projective space, the Galois action factors through the cyclotomic character.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Let $F$ be a number field, and let $X$ be an elliptic curve over $F$. Then the Tate module $\mathrm H^1_{\mathrm{\acute et}}(X_{\overline F};\ZZ_p)$ is enough to recover $X$ up to isogeny, by a theorem of Siegel.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Let $Y$ be a smooth proper curve over a number field $F$, and define $X$ to be the scheme $Y$ glued together at two rational points $x$ and $y$. Then there is an extension
	\[0\to\ZZ_p\to\mathrm H^1_{\mathrm{\acute et}}(X_{\overline F};\ZZ_p)\to\mathrm H^1_{\mathrm{\acute et}}(Y_{\overline F};\ZZ_p)\to0.\] 
	Even though the left and right terms do not depend on the choice of points, the extension class depends exactly on $[x]-[y]\in\op{Pic}(Y)/\op{Pic}(Y)_{\mathrm{tors}}$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Symplectic Groups}
Now, we are interested in the homotopy type $\widehat{\mathrm B\op{GL}_n(\ZZ)}$. But this turns out to not be the \'etale homotopy type of any smooth algebraic variety as soon as $n\ge3$. To fix this, we avoid working with linear groups and instead work with symplectic groups.
\begin{notation}
	Let $\mc A_g$ be the moduli stack of principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension $g$.
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	We can realize $\mc A_g$ as the orbifold $\HH_g/\op{Sp}_{2g}(\ZZ)$, where $\HH_g$ is the Siegel space. Thus, $\mc A_g(\CC)$ is $\mathrm B\op{Sp}_{2g}(\ZZ)$. It follows that we receive an interesting Galois action on $\widehat{\mathrm B\op{Sp}_{2g}(\ZZ)}$.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}
	There is a non-split extension
	\[0\to\QQ_p(-1)\to V\to\QQ_p\to0\]
	of Galois representations such that $V$ embeds into $\op{Hom}_{\mathrm{cts}}(\pi_2^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(\mc A_{g,\ov\QQ}),\QQ_p)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	This $V$ is a genuinely new Galois representation: one cannot $V$ as a subquotient of any ``geometric'' Galois representation, namely coming from $\mathrm H^n_{\mathrm{\acute et}}(X_{\overline\QQ};\QQ_p)$. The reason is due to Hodge--Tate weights: the quotient $\QQ_p$ of $V$ has weight $0$ while the source $\QQ_p(-1)$ has weight $-1$, and there are no such non-split Galois representations coming from geometry.
\end{remark}
Let's give retell the story of our motivation for symplectic groups. One has
\[\lim_n\mathrm H^*(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ);\QQ_p)\cong\QQ_p[x_2,x_6,x_{10},\ldots],\]
where the subscripts denote degrees. These come from some Chern classes. On the other hand,
\[\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ);\QQ_p)=\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\widehat\ZZ);\QQ_p)\cong\land^*(b_3,b_7,\ldots).\]
Thus, the natural map from the second algebra to the first one must vanish for degree reasons! It follows that we expect many interesting homotopy groups. Here is what we expect.
\begin{conj}
	For each $i$, the direct system
	\[\pi_i^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(\mc A_{g,\ov\QQ})\to\pi_i^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(\mc A_{g+1,\ov\QQ})\to\pi_i^{\mathrm{\acute et}}(\mc A_{g+2,\ov\QQ})\to\cdots\]
	stabilizes to $0$ if $i\not\equiv2\pmod4$; otherwise, if $i=4k+2$, it stabilizes to a Galois representation $V_k$ fitting in an extension
	\[0\to\QQ_p\to V_k\to\QQ_p(2k+1)\to0.\]
\end{conj}
\begin{remark}
	Again, these extensions $V_k$ fail to be geometric.
\end{remark}
Here is what we can prove.
\begin{theorem}[Pan--S]
	Fix a smooth algebraic variety $X$ over $\CC$, and suppose that we have a representation $\pi_1(X(\CC))\to\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ_p)$. Then the map
	\[\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ_p);\QQ_p)\to\mathrm H^*(X(\CC);\QQ_p)\]
	vanishes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	There is an analogous statement for the group $\op{SL}_n$ instead of $\op{Sp}_{2n}$.
\end{remark}
Instead of explaining anything about this proof, we give the following example.
\begin{example}
	Let's prove this for $X=\mc A_{g,\CC}$, without using the calcualtions from earlier. Then there is a natural monodromy representation $\rho\colon\pi_1(X(\CC))\to\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ_p)$, so we get a natural map
	\[\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{cts}}(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ_p);\QQ_p)\to\mathrm H^*_{\mathrm{\acute et}}(\mc A_{g,\ov\QQ};\QQ_p).\]
	This map is Galois-equivariant because the representation is defined over $\QQ$. It then follows by looking at some Hodge--Tate weights that the target has no nonzero invaraints (e.g., one can use the Weil conjectures), but the Galois action on the source is trivial, so the actual map must vanish.
\end{example}
While we're here, let's explain how we can say anything about the Galois action.
\begin{theorem}
	The natural map
	\[\mathrm R\Gamma(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ_p);\QQ_p)\to\mathrm R\Gamma(\op{Sp}_{2n}(\ZZ);\QQ_p)\]
	is Galois-equivariant and nonzero in the derived category. In fact, this map is given by certain explicit classes from $V_k$s as constructed in the conjecture.
\end{theorem}

\section{April 14: The Spectral Category}
This talk was given by Mohit at MIT for the categorical local Langlands seminar.

\subsection{Working with \texorpdfstring{$\op{IndCoh}$}{ IndCoh}}
We are interested in proving an equivalence of categories like the following.
\begin{theorem}
	There is an equivalence
	\[\op{Shv}^{\widehat{\mathrm{unip}}}(\mathrm{Isoc}_G,\ov\QQ_\ell)\to\op{IndCoh}(\mathrm{Loc}_{^cG,F}^{\widehat{\mathrm{unip}}}\otimes\ov\QQ_\ell).\]
\end{theorem}
The idea is to write both sides as the categorical trace of some categories; we did this for the left-hand side for the past few weeks, so it is now time to do it for the right-hand side. Notably, we have yet to receive a good definition of the right-hand side.

Let's start by defining coherent sheaves.
\begin{notation}
	For today, a \textit{stack} is an algebraic stack of almost finite presentation over a coefficient ring $\Lambda$. Simialrly, a \textit{space} is an algebraic space which is almost finite presentation over $\Lambda$.
\end{notation}
To start, note that we already have a functor $\mathrm{Mod}\colon\mathrm{CAlg}_\Lambda\to\mathrm{LinCat}_\Lambda$. One can extend this to quasicoherent sheaves by taking a Kan extension.
\begin{definition}[quasicoherent sheaf]
	For a prestack $X$, we define its category of \textit{quasicoherent sheaves} as
	\[\op{QCoh}(X)\coloneqq\lim_{\Spec A\to X}\mathrm{Mod}(A).\]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It follows from the construction that $\op{QCoh}(X)$ has a $t$-structure, an exterior product $\boxtimes$, and $*$-pullbacks and $*$-pushforwards for morphisms representable in algebraic spaces.
\end{remark}
Similarly, we have perfect sheaves.
\begin{definition}[perfect]
	For an algebra $A$, we let $\op{Perf}(A)$ be the thick subcategory of $\mathrm{Mod}(A)$ generated by $A$. For a prestack $X$, we define
	\[\op{QCoh}(X)=\lim_{\Spec A\to X}\op{Perf}(X).\]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	When $X$ is an algebraic space, $\op{IndPerf}(X)=\op{QCoh}(X)$. In general, $\op{Perf}(X)$ is the dualizable objects in $\op{QCoh}(X)$.
\end{remark}
This allows us to define coherent sheaves.
\begin{definition}[coherent]
	For a stack $X$, we define $\op{Coh}(X)$ to be those quasicoherent sheaves $\mc F$ such that $\mc H^i(\mc F)$ is coherent on the underlying classical stack of $X$ and zero almost everywhere. We then define $\op{IndCoh}(X)$ to be the ind-objects of $\op{Coh}(X)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	One has that $\mc H^0(\OO_X)$ is just the structure sheaf on the underlying classical stack.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
	There is an inclusion $\op{Coh}(X)\to\op{QCoh}(X)$, so one gets an embedding $\op{IndCoh}(X)\to\op{QCoh}(X)$. This turns out to given an equivalence on bounded objects.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	There is an action of $\op{Perf}(X)$ on $\op{Coh}(X)$, which extends to an action for the ind-categories. If $\op{Perf}(X)\subseteq\op{Coh}(X)$ (this amounts to requiring that $\OO_X$ is coherent), then we actually receive a map $\op{IndPerf}(X)\to\op{IndCoh}(X)$.
\end{remark}
We will want some functoriality for $\op{IndCoh}$.
\begin{remark}
	Given a map $f\colon X\to Y$, the map $f_*\colon\op{Coh}(X)\to\op{QCoh}(Y)$ actually maps to objects with bounded homotopy groups, whic is $\op{QCoh}(Y)^+$. This is then equivalent to $\op{IndCoh}(Y)^+$, which embeds into $\op{IndCoh}(Y)$. Taking ind-completion, we receive a functor
	\[f_*\colon\op{IndCoh}(X)\to\op{IndCoh}(Y).\]
	If $f$ has ``finite tor amplitude,'' then there is an adjoint functor $f_*$; this means that $f^*\colon\op{QCoh}(Y)^{\ge0}\to\op{QCoh}(X)^{\ge n}$ for some $n$.
\end{remark}
The previous remark motivates us to upgrade $\op{IndCoh}$ to a functor.
\begin{theorem}
	There is a functor $\op{IndCoh}^*$ from the category of correspondences on ind-stacks (where the pullback morphism needs to exist) to $\op{LinCat}_\Lambda(X)$. Here,
	\[\op{IndCoh}^*(\colim X_\bullet)\coloneqq\colim\op{IndCoh}^*(X_\bullet),\]
	where the latter colimit is taken for $*$-pushforwards of closed embeddings.
\end{theorem}
Here is some more functoriality.
\begin{remark}
	Given $X$ and $Y$, the exterior product $\boxtimes\colon\op{QCoh}(X)\times\op{QCoh}(Y)\to\op{QCoh}(X\times Y)$ restricts to coherent sheaves and then completes to
	\[\boxtimes\op{IndCoh}(X)\otimes_\Lambda\op{IndCoh}(Y)\to\op{IndCoh}(X\times Y).\]
	This is fully faithful, and one can check that it is an equivalence by stratifying. The idea is to use some excision for $\op{IndCoh}$.
\end{remark}
With some $*$-functors, we will want some $!$-functors.
\begin{remark}
	Every morphism $f\colon X\to Y$ of algebraic spaces admits a compactification $X\into\ov X\to Y$. One shows this by passing to schemes. An adjoint functor theorem shows that $(-)^!$ exists for the proper morphisms, so taking the composite defines $f^!$.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}
	There is a funtor $\op{IndCoh}^!$ from the category of correspondences of ind-stacks (where the morphisms are representable in ind-spaces) to $\op{LinCat}_\Lambda$. It sends the correspondence $X\stackrel f\from Z\stackrel g\to Y$ to $g_*f^!$. It turns out that $\op{IndCoh}^!(X)=\op{IndCoh}^*(X)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
	There is content in the statement $\op{IndCoh}^!(X)=\op{IndCoh}^*(X)$. Indeed, one should define this for ind-stacks to
	\[\lim\op{IndCoh}(X_\bullet),\]
	where the latter limit is taken over $!$-pullbacks.
\end{remark}
We now have enough functors to do some interesting things.
\begin{remark}
	The category $\op{IndCoh}^!$ is symmetric monoidal with tensor product
	\[\mc F\otimes^!\mc G\coloneqq\Delta^!(\mc F\boxtimes\mc G).\]
	The unit is the dualizing sheaf. This turns out to give a duality $\mathbb D$ on $\op{IndCoh}(X)$, recovering Grothendieck duality.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	If $f\colon X\to Y$ is representable in ind-spaces, then $\op{IndCoh}(X\times_YX)$ admits a convolution structure. If $\boxtimes$ is an equivalence for the product $X\times_YX$ (with itself), then the convolution has some duality.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Steinberg Stack}
We are now allowed to write down the expression $\op{IndCoh}(\op{Loc}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{unip}})$. Eventually, we will want to show an equivalence with this category, so we need to be able to find some interesting objects.
\begin{example}
	Here are some ``constant objects.'' There is a natural map $f\colon\op{Loc}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{unip}}\to\mathrm B\widehat G$, so we can pull back perfect objects in the target to perfect objects in the source. These turn out to give objects in $\op{IndCoh}$.
\end{example}
Let's find some more objects. We will go through the following intermediate object.
\begin{definition}[Steinberg stack]
	We define the \textit{Steinberg stack} as
	\[\mc S^{\mathrm{unip}}_{^cG,\check F}\coloneqq\mathrm{Loc}_{^cB,\check F}^{\mathrm{unip}}\times_{\mathrm{Loc}_{^cG,\check F}}\mathrm{Loc}^{\mathrm{unip}}_{\check B,\check F}\]
	There is also a tame variant.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	There is a resolution
	\[\mc S^{\mathrm{unip}}_{^cG,\check F}\cong\left(\widetilde U_{\widehat G}\times_{\widehat G}\widetilde U_{\widehat G}\right)/\widehat G,\]
	where $\widetilde U_{\widehat G}$ is the unipotent variety. This gives many nice geometric properties of the Steinberg stack; for example, it admits a stratification from a stratification of the unipotent variety.
\end{remark}
We still need a way to mediate from the Steinberg stack.
\begin{definition}
	We define
	\[\widetilde{\mathrm{Loc}}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{tame}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Loc}_{^cB,\check F}^{\mathrm{tame}}\times_{\mathrm{Loc}^{\mathrm{tame}}_{^cG,\check F}}\mathrm{Loc}^{\mathrm{tame}}_{^cG,F}.\]
	There is a slightly more complicated definition in the unipotent case.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	There is a natural projection $\widetilde{\mathrm{Loc}}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{tame}}\to\mathrm{Loc}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{tame}}$. It is harder to define, but there is also a map $\delta$ to $\mc S^{\mathrm{tame}}_{G,\check F}$. For brevity, define $X\coloneqq\op{Loc}_{B,\check F}^{\mathrm{tame}}$ and $Y\coloneqq\op{Loc}_{G,\check F}^{\mathrm{tame}}$, and then
	\[\widetilde{\op{Loc}}_{G}^{\mathrm{tame}}=X\times_Y\mc L_\varphi(Y)=X\times_Y(Y\times_YY)=X\times_{Y\times Y}Y,\]
	which then maps via diagonal to $(X\times X)\times_{Y\times Y}Y=X\times_YX$, which is $\mc S_G^{\mathrm{tame}}$. In short, this map $\delta$ can be given as $x\mapsto(x,\varphi(x))$.
\end{remark}
Thus, we see that $\pi_*\delta^!$ can give us many interesting objects.
\begin{definition}[Deligne--Lusztig induction]
	We define \textit{Deligne--Lusztig induction} $\mathrm{Ch}_{^cG}^{\mathrm{tame}}\coloneqq\pi_*\delta^!$ to be the functor
	\[\pi_*\delta^!\colon\op{IndCoh}(\mc S^{\mathrm{tame}}_{^cG,\check F})\to\op{IndCoh}(\op{Loc}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{tame}}).\]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	It turns out that the essential iamge of $\op{Ch}$ is contained in $\op{IndCoh}(\mathrm{Loc}_{^cG,F}^{\mathrm{unip}})$ and stable under the action of $\op{IndPerf}$.
\end{remark}
We are now interested in understanding the Steinberg stack better.
\begin{proposition}
	Let $S$ be the Steinberg stack. Then
	\[\boxtimes\colon\op{IndCoh}(S)\otimes\op{IndCoh}(Y)\to\op{IndCoh}(S\times Y).\]
\end{proposition}
One shows this by reducing to the case of $Y=S$, where one can check it via explicit calculation on tangent spaces and things.
\begin{remark}
	Taking $Y=S$ reveals that $\op{IndCoh}(S)$ admits a duality $\mathbb D^\star$ coming from convolution (because then $\op{IndCoh}(S)$ becomes semi-rigid with a unit). It turns out that this is the ``swap'' of the original duality $\mathbb D$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	The dualities almost commute with $\op{Ch}$: one has $\mathbb D\circ\mathrm{Ch}=\mathrm{Ch}\circ\mathrm{swap}\circ\mathbb D^\star$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Motivation}
Here is our motivating result.
\begin{theorem}[Gross--Zagier]
	Fix an elliptic curve $E$ over $\QQ$, and choose a large prime $p$. Then if the analytic rank of $E$ is in $\{0,1\}$, then the algebraic rank equals the analytic rank, and $\#\Sha(E/\QQ)[p^\infty]$ is finite.

This was shown in two steps. Choose an imaginary quadratic extension $K/\QQ$ satisfying some Heegner hypothesis on ramification.
\begin{itemize}

	\item Kolyvagin showed that the Heegner point having infinite order implies that $\op{Sel}_{p^\infty}(E/K)$ has rank $1$.
\end{itemize}
We are interested in showing some cases of the Bloch--Kato conjecture for groups of higher rank. The idea is to allow our objects vary $p$-adically and combine an Iwasawa main conjecture with a calculation of $p$-adic $L$-functions. The problem is that the value of a $p$-adic $L$-value may have a difficult time actually recovering an $L$-value at the end. However, taking further $p$-adic variation allows us to find the $L$-values.

\subsection{Back to Categorical Trace}
We work with $\Lambda=\ov\QQ_\ell$ for simplicity. Here is some formalism.
\begin{definition}
	Let $A$ be an algebra object in a stable category $R$, and let $F$ be an $A$-bimodule. Then we define the \textit{trace}
	\[\op{Tr}(A,F)\coloneqq A\otimes_{A\otimes A\opp}F,\]
	which is basically some Hochschild cohomology. For an endomorphism $\varphi$ of $A$, then $\op{Tr}(A,\varphi)\coloneqq\op{Tr}(A,{^\varphi A})$, where $^\varphi A$ is the $A$-bimodule whose left action is twisted by $\varphi$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $A$ is an ordinary $k$-algebra, then the trace is the quotient of $M$ by the submodule generated by the elements $am-ma$.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}
	Let $A$ be an algebra object in a stable category $R$, and let $F$ be an $A$-bimodule. Then we define the \textit{center} as $\op{Hom}_{A\otimes A\opp}(A,F)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[singular support]

\subsection{The Bernstein Center}
Here is a general construction.
\begin{definition}[Bernstein center]
	Let $\mc C$ be a rigid $\otimes$-category. Then we define its \textit{center} $Z(\mc C)$ as the endomorphisms of the identity natural transformation.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}[Bernstein center]

\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Note that $Z(G)$ acts by a scalar on each irreducible representation. Thus, for a finite group $G$, we find that $Z(G)$ is a product of $\CC$s, with a number of factors equal to the number of irreducible representations of $G$.
\end{example}
One can also view the Bernstein center coming from distributions.
\begin{definition}[distribution]

\end{definition}
\begin{remark}

\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
	The box product gives $\op{Dist}_c(G)$ an algebra structure.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}[Bernstein]

\end{theorem}

\end{example}

\subsection{Comparing Descents}
Throughout, $k$ is a number field, and $X$ is a smooth projective geometrically connected variety.
\begin{notation}
	Fix a variety $X$ over a number field $k$. Then we define
	\[X(\AA_k)_\bullet\coloneqq\prod_{v\nmid\infty}X(k_v)\times\prod_{v\mid\infty}\pi_0(X(k_v)).\]
\end{notation}
Here is our target.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a smooth projective geometrically connected curve $X$ over a global field $k$. Then the finite abelian descent obstruction and the Brauer--Manin obstructions are the same.
\end{theorem}
Let's start by reviewing the descent obstruction.
\begin{proposition}
	Fix a finite abelian group scheme $G$ over a field $k$. For any variety $X$ over a field $k$, the group $\mathrm H^1(X;G)$ classifies $G$-torsors over $X$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{definition}
	Fix a $G$-torsor $Y\to X$. Then $p\in X(\AA_k)_\bullet$ \textit{survives $Y$} if and only if it lifts to a point in some twist $Y_\xi(\AA_k)_\bullet$ for some $\xi\in\mathrm H^1(k;G)$. We define $X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{fab}}$ to be the set of points which survive all torsors for all finite abelian groups.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	One can show that $X(k)\subseteq X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{fab}}$.
\end{remark}
And we should also review the Brauer--Manin obstruction. We need the following result from class field theory.
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a local field $k_v$. Then there is a natural embedding $\op{inv}_v\colon\op{Br}k_v\to\QQ/\ZZ$, which is surjective if $v$ is finite, has image $\frac12\ZZ/\ZZ$ if $v$ is real, and has image $0$ if $v$ is complex.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a global field $k$. Then localizations provide the exact sequence
	\[0\to\op{Br}k\to\bigoplus_v\op{Br}k_v\to\QQ/\ZZ\to0,\]
	where the last map sums $\op{inv}_v$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{notation}
	We define a pairing $X(\AA_k)_\bullet\times\op{Br}k\to\QQ/\ZZ$ by
	\[\langle x,b\rangle\coloneqq\sum_v\op{inv}_v\op{ev}_{x_v}(b),\]
	where $\op{ev}_{x_v}$ is the torsor induced by the composite map $\Spec k_v\to X\to\mathrm BG$.
\end{notation}
\begin{remark}
	For any $x\in X(k)$, we have $\op{ev}_x\colon\op{Br}X\to\op{Br}k$, and we can see that $\langle x,b\rangle=0$ for all $b$ because $\op{ev}_x(b)\in\op{Br}k$.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
	We define the \textit{Brauer--Manin set} to be $X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{Br}}$ to be the set of $x\in X(\AA_k)$ for which $\langle x,b\rangle=0$ for all $b\in\op{Br}X$.
\end{definition}
We can now restate our main theorem.
\begin{theorem}[Stoll]
	Fix a smooth projective geometrically connected curve $X$ over a global field $k$. Then $X(k)^{\mathrm{fab}}=X(k)^{\mathrm{Br}}$.
\end{theorem}
One of the inclusions does not need to use the curve hypothesis.
\begin{theorem}[Skorobogatov]
	Fix a smooth projective geometrically connected curve $X$ over a global field $k$. Then
	\[X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{Br}}\subseteq X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{Br}_1}\subseteq X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{fab}}.\]
	Here, $\op{Br}_1$ is the kernel of the natural map $\op{Br}X\to\op{Br}X_{\overline k}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Choose $x\in X(\AA_k)^{\mathrm{Br}_1}$. For any finite abelian group scheme $S$ over $k$ and $S$-torsor $Y\to X$, we would like to find $\sigma\in\mathrm H^1(k;S)$ such that $x$ lifts to $Y_\sigma(\AA_k)$. To this end, we define $M\coloneqq\op{Hom}_{\overline k}(S,\mathbb G_m)$ to be the dual group, which is a finite discrete $\op{Gal}(\ov k/k)$-module.
	
	By spreading out, we note that there is a finite set place $S$ of places for which $M$ is unramified outside $S$, so we can find an integral model $\mathbb S\coloneqq\op{Hom}(M,\mathbb G_m)$ of $S$. By further expanding $T$, we may assume that $Y\to X$ becoms an $\mathcal S$-torsor $\mc Y\to\mc X$, and we may assume that $x_v\in\mc X(\OO_v)$ for all $v\notin T$. Now, we can pull back $\mc Y\to\mc X$ along $x_v\colon\Spec k_v\to\mc X$ to produce an $\mathcal S$-torsor $\mc Y_{x_v}\to\Spec k_v$. By construction, the collection of torsors $(\mc Y_{x_v})_v$ provide a class in
	\[P^1(k;S)\coloneqq\prod_{v\in T}\mathrm H^1(k_v;S)\times\prod_{v\notin T}\mathrm H^1(\OO_v;S_v).\]
	It is enough to show that the collection of torsors $(\mc Y_{x_v})_v$ come from a single class $\sigma\in\mathrm H^1(k;S)$, for then we can simultaneously trivialize all these torsors by twisting by $\sigma$. To this end, recall from global duality that there is an exact sequence
	\[\mathrm H^1(k;S)\to P^1(k;S)\to\mathrm H^1(k;M)^*,\]
	where the last map is the composite of the sum of the local maps
	\[\mathrm H^1(k_v;S)\cong\mathrm H^1(k_v;M)^*\stackrel{\op{res}^*}\to\mathrm H^1(k;M)^*.\]
	Thus, we would like to show that the image of $(\mc Y_{x_v})_v$ vanishes in $\mathrm H^1(k;M)^*$, which is just some calculation with invariants.
\end{proof}

\end{definition}
It may be convenient to take moduli.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a prime $\ell$. Then a \textit{mod-$\ell$ cusp form} is a power series of the form

\begin{definition}[Hecke operators]
	There is an algebra $\mathbb T$ of commuting endomorphisms given by $\{T_p\}_{p\text{ prime}}$ acting on $\overline S(N,k,\varepsilon,\ell)$. If $p\nmid N$, then the Hecke operator lifts to $S(N,k,\varepsilon)$.
\end{definition}

	If $f$ is a Hecke eigenform, then it can be normalized so that $a_{1,f}=1$. It then turns out that $T_pf=a_{p,f}f$, and one finds that these are all elements of $\overline\ZZ$, so we produce a mod-$\ell$ modular form. \begin{definition}[modular curve]
	The \textit{modular curve} is some curve $X_0(N)$ defined over $\QQ$ which is isomorphic to $\mc H/\Gamma_0(N)$ over $\CC$. It basically parameterizes elliptic curvess with a choice of cyclic subgroup of size $N$.

\begin{remark}
	By definition, a cusp form $f$ of weight two induces a holomorphic differential form on $X_0(N)$.
\end{remark}

	Set $J_0(N)\coloneqq\op{Jac}X_0(N)$. For any Hecke eigenform $f$, let $\lambda_f\colon\mathbb T\to\CC$ denote the eigenvalue of $f$, and we define
	\[A_f\coloneqq J_0(N)/(\ker\lambda_f).\]
\end{notation}

\begin{theorem}[Eichler--Shimura]
	Fix a Hecke eigenform $f$, and let $\lambda_f\colon\mathbb G\to\CC$ be the eigenvalue. If $\lambda_f$ outputs to $\ZZ$, then $A_f$ is an elliptic curve. In fact,
	\[\tr(\mathrm{Frob}_p;T_\ell A_f)=\lambda_f(T_p)\]
	for all primes $p$ of good reduction, distinct from $\ell$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Serre's Conjecture}
The following was conjectured by Serre before 1987 and proven only in the 2000s.
\begin{definition}[odd]
	Fix a modular Galois representation $\ov\rho_\ell\colon\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ/\QQ)\to\op{GL}_n(\FF_\ell)$. Let $c\subseteq\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ/\QQ)$ be the conjugacy class of complex conjugation. Then we say that $\ov\rho_\ell$ is \textit{odd} if and only if $\det\ov\rho_\ell(c)=-1$.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
	If $f$ is a Hecke eigenform, then $\overline\rho_{f,\ell}$ is odd. Indeed, for an elliptic curve $\CC/\Lambda$, complex conjugation acts with determinant $-1$ on the lattice $\Lambda\subseteq\CC$, so it acts by $-1$ on the torsion and thus also on the Tate module. Alternatively, 
\end{example}
\begin{theorem}[Khare--Winterberger]
	Fix a modular Galois representation $\ov\rho_\ell\colon\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ/\QQ)\to\op{GL}_2(\FF_\ell)$. If $\ov\rho_\ell$ is absolutely irreducible and odd, then there is a mod-$\ell$ Hecke eigenform $\overline f$ such that $\overline\rho_\ell=\overline\rho_{\overline f,\ell}$.
\end{theorem}
We won't prove this; it's very hard. Here is an application.
\begin{corollary}
	Fix a prime $\ell\ge5$. Then
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	Suppose $(a,b,c)$ is a solution. Define the elliptic curve $E$ by
	\[y^2=x\left(x+a^\ell\right)\left(x-b^\ell\right).\]
	We now consider the Galois representation $\overline\rho_{E,\ell}$. Its discriminant is some power of $2$ times $a^\ell b^\ell c^\ell$, but any primes appearing in $a^\ell b^\ell c^\ell$ needs to appear with multiplicity of $\ell$. Work of Serre is able to remove these extra primes from the conductor, so $N(\rho_{\ell,E})=2$ and $k=2$, but there are no modular forms with these criteria.
\end{proof}
For our main application, we will need the following definition.
\begin{definition}[locally elliptic family]
	Fix a finite set $S$ of primes. A \textit{locally elliptic family of Galois representations} is a tuple $\{\rho_\ell\}_\ell$ of Galois representations $\rho_\ell\colon\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ/\QQ)\to\op{GL}_2(\ZZ_\ell)$ such that each $p\notin S$ admits an elliptic curve $E_p/\QQ_p$ with good reduction and for which
	\[\rho_\ell|_{\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ_p/\QQ_p)}\cong\rho_{\ell,E_p}\]
	for all $\ell$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}
	Fix a finite set $S$ of primes and a locally elliptic family of Galois representations $\{\rho_\ell\}_\ell$. Then there is an elliptic curve $E$ over $\QQ$ such that $\rho_{E,\ell}=\rho_\ell$ for all $\ell$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	For each prime $\ell$, let $N_\ell$, $k_\ell$, and $\varepsilon_\ell$ be the usual invariants for $\overline\rho_\ell$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Let's start by showing that $k_\ell=2$ for $\ell\notin S$. Well, for $\ell\notin S$, Serre's work shows that $k_\ell$ only depends on $\overline\rho_\ell|_{\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ_\ell/\QQ_\ell)}$, but this is $\overline\rho_{\ell,E_\ell}$, which then has $k_\ell=2$ already.

		\item We claim there is some $N$ for which there is a positive density set of $\ell\notin S$ such that $N_\ell\mid N$. This is a rather lengthy calculation. Having good reduction everywhere implies that $N_\ell$ is always supported in $S$. Accordingly, fix some $p\in S$, and we will bound the exponent of $p$ dividing $N_\ell$; we would like to show that it is uniformly bounded in $\ell$ and almost always zero.
		
		Well, define $G_1$ to be the image of $\rho_\ell$ along the image of wild inertia $I_p^1\subseteq\op{Gal}(\overline\QQ/\QQ)$. By work of Serre, the exponent of $p$ dividing $N_\ell$ is bounded in terms of $\#G_1$, but $G_1\subseteq\op{GL}_2(\FF_\ell)$, which is some function of $\ell$. Thus, we are hunting for $p$-subgroups of $\op{GL}_2(\FF_\ell)$, but if $p$ fails to divide $\#\op{GL}_2(\FF_\ell)$, then $G_1$ must be trivial, and we get our uniform bound. We can ensure that $p\nmid\#\op{GL}_2(\FF_\ell)$ by adding some congruence conditions to $\ell$, so we are done!

		\item For all but finitely many $\ell$, we claim that $\overline\rho_\ell$ is irreducible. Let's take $\ell\notin S$. Well, $\overline\rho_\ell$ is irreducible if and only if $E_\ell$ has supersingular good reduction because we need to kill $\ell$-torsion to get irreducibility.

		Suppose that $\overline\rho_\ell$ reduces for infinitely many primes $\ell$. Then $\overline\rho_\ell$ divides up into two characters, and by the Weil pairing, these two characters look like $\psi\oplus\chi\psi^{-1}$, where $\chi$ is the cyclotomic character. Thus, for $p\equiv1\pmod N$ where $N$ is the conductor, we can find that $a_{p,E_\ell}$ can be calculated as the trace of the Frobenius, which is $p+1\pmod\ell$ (because $\psi$ is trivial on $\op{Frob}_p$). This cannot vanish for infinitely many $\ell$, so we are done.

		\item Now, we can use Serre's conjecture on the previous two steps to find a Hecke eigenform $f$ with $a_{p,\overline f}=a_{p,E_p}$ for $p\notin S$, which will complete the proof.

		Indeed, Serre's conjecture gives $f_\ell$ for infinitely many primes $\ell$ for which $a_{p,E_\ell}=a_{p,\rho_\ell}$. Some $f$ appears infinitely often, and this is enough to conclude by making an argument with the Euler product.
		\qedhere
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Boundedness of Special Points}
Throughout, we fix a smooth projective family $f\colon X\to S$ of algebraic varieties over a number field $K$; in fact, in our applications, $S$ will always be a curve, and $X$ will be a family of surfaces. We would like to understand the set of points $\mc S\subseteq S(\overline K)$ over which the fibers have some extra structure.
\begin{example}
	Let $X\to S$ be the Legendre family $y^2=x(x-1)(x-\lambda)$ of elliptic curves over $S\coloneqq\ZZ[\lambda,1/\lambda,1/(\lambda-1)]$. Then we could let $\mathcal S$ be the locus where the elliptic curve has complex multiplication.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	Let $f\colon X\to S$ be a family of projective surfaces over a curve $S$. One could consider the locus $\mc S_k$ of points $\overline s$ where the fiber has
	\[\op{rank}\op{NS}(X_{\overline\eta})=\op{rank}\op{NS}(X_{\overline s})-k.\]
	Namely, we are studying exceptional points with $k$ ``extra'' line bundles. When this is a family of K3 surfaces, it is known that $\mc S_1$ is dense and even equidistributed (by viewing these as variations of Hodge structures), and one expects that $\mc S_2$ is usually finite.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$G$}{ G}-functions}
Our main arithmetic input comes from $G$-functions.
\begin{definition}
	Fix a number field $K$. A \textit{$G$-function} is a formal power series $G\in K[[s]]$ satisfying the following.
	\begin{listalph}
		\item Differential equation: the power series $G$ satisfies a linear differential equation
		\[a_\ell\frac{d^\ell G}{ds^\ell}+\cdots+a_1\frac{dG}{ds}=0,\]
		where $a_i\in K(s)$ for each $i$.
		\item Integrality: if $G=\sum_{i\ge0}b_is^i$, then there are integers $\{c_i\}$ such that $\{c_ib_i\}_{i\ge0}$ is integral, and $\left|c_i\right|\le\kappa^i$ for some fixed $\kappa$.
	\end{listalph}
\end{definition}
We are interested in the relationship between $G$-functions.
\begin{definition}[global relation]
	Fix a $G$-function $G$ over a number field $K$. Then a place $v$ of $K$ is \textit{relevant} for $\xi\in\overline K$ and $G$ if and only if $\left|\xi\right|_v$ is less than the $v$-adic radius of convergence of $G$. THen a polynoimal $P$ is a \textit{global relation} at a fixed $\xi\in\overline K$ of $G$-functions $\{G_1,\ldots,G_m\}$ if and only if
	\[P(G_1(\xi),\ldots,G_m(\xi))=0\]
	for all relevant places $v$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	The $G$-functions tend to have radius of convergence $1$ by the integrality hypotheses, so one expects to have only finitely many relevant places.
\end{remark}
\begin{theorem}[Bombieri]
	Fix $G$-functions $\{G_1,\ldots,G_m\}$ over a number field $K$. There are positive constants $a$ and $b$ such that all $\xi\in\overline K$ admitting a nontrivial global relation $P_\xi$ have
	\[h(\xi)\le a(\log P_\xi)^b.\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Some Explicit Bounds}
For today, we let $a_\bullet\colon\ZZ^+\to\CC$ be an arithmetic function, and we are interested in bounding the partial sums
\[\sum_{n\le x}a_n.\]
Usually, our arithmetic functions will have Dirichlet series
\[A(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{a_n}{n^s}\]
with some meromorphic continuation.
\begin{example}
	With
	\[a_n\coloneqq\begin{cases}
		\log p & \text{if }n=p^k\text{ for some prime }p, \\
		0 & \text{otherwise}.
	\end{cases}\]
	Then $A(s)=-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)$. Zero-free regions of $\zeta(s)$ prove the Prime number theorem. In the sequel, we will denote $\psi(x)\coloneqq A(x)$.
\end{example}
\begin{example}
	With $a_n\coloneqq\mu(n)$, we have $A(s)=1/\zeta(s)$. In the sequel, we will denote $M(x)\coloneqq A(X)$. The Prime number theorem is equivalent to $M(x)=o(x)$.
\end{example}
The bounds on $\left|\psi(x)-x\right|$ are much better than those on $\left|M(x)\right|$. Here are some reasons why.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Estimating $\left|\psi(x)-x\right|$ basically needs to know where the zeroes are (to control $-\zeta'/\zeta$), but estimating $M(x)$ requires one need to know some residues (to control $1/\zeta$). If zeroes are close together (which is difficult to control), then such residues can explode.
	\item It is difficult to only use finite information about residues in order to produce bounds on $M(x)$.
\end{enumerate}
The main result of the present talk is to explain how to use finite informatino about residues to produce bounds.
